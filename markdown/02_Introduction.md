Purple belt made threads and systems real to me: where I could go from a position, where my opponent could go, and how to apply positions so my will became easier to execute while theirs became harder. Brown belt forced me to unlearn and relearn more than any other belt. Now as a black belt, I feel confident in my offense, confident in my defense, confident in my strategy, confident in my understanding, and confident that I have so much to learn and re-learn.

Teaching changed the way I understand the art. I started teaching no-gi fundamentals as a blue belt and continued through purple, forcing me to unpack positions, techniques, counters, escapes, and movements through the eyes of someone who might know nothing about them. I had to dissect things into granular pieces and communicate those pieces. I did not want to teach only steps; I wanted to explain the "why" up front. I'm a tech nerd professionally, and in both technology and jiu jitsu, I am drawn to identifying and isolating the "cotter pin": the small piece that holds the thing together (a cotter pin is the small part that keeps a wheel on its axle). If you can identify that point and convey it accurately, people absorb the material faster.

That way of thinking started long before jiu jitsu. I grew up in the Midwest, mostly in small towns around Springfield, Illinois. I was a small kid in an area filled with farmers and blue-collar workers (steak, potatoes, corn-fed, and strong), and I had a loud mouth. I got into fewer fights than I probably should have. I also got my first computer when I was six, a Commodore 64, and learned BASIC from a book that mixed programming fundamentals with children's games. It consumed me. Looking back, that early exposure to technology shaped how I dissect, distill, and apply concepts. Jiu jitsu became another live system to understand.

A technique by itself, though, is only an answer to a very narrow, particular version of a problem. The moment the opponent changes their posture, changes their angle, hides an elbow, shifts their hips, or removes a grip, the memorized answer may no longer fit. Good students often run into this problem first. They are paying attention and trying to do what they were taught, but the move that worked during drilling disappears during sparring, and they do not know whether they missed a detail or misunderstood the position. Most of the time, both are true.

Jiu jitsu is too alive to be learned as a stack of technique recipes. The details matter, but the details sit on top of conditions. A pass succeeds because distance, angle, posture, grips, and hip mobility have been addressed. A sweep lands once base has been compromised, a post has been removed or overloaded, and a fulcrum has been established over which movement can be created; a submission finishes when the defender has been logically separated into smaller, weaker parts. When students learn only the visible move, they often chase the ending before they have created the beginning.

In a closed-guard class, we were looking at the mindset of the person on top and the person on bottom through the lens of a simple scissor sweep (I say "lens" a lot). The bottom player controlled the top player's left sleeve, so the hand post on that side was gone. On paper, that should make the sweep available. The top player solved it by extending the left knee outward and posting with the knee instead. The arm was controlled, but the quadrant was still defended (the Quadrants chapter goes through this in detail). Once the bottom player understood that, the answer was not to force the sweep with more effort, but to break posture, load the top player's weight onto their hip, and pull them toward the upper right, bypassing the knee post entirely.

This book is about moments like that one, when a better way to see the problem helps a student more than a new move would.

My interest in concepts came from wanting simpler explanations on the mat. I wanted students to have answers to questions they could actually use while they were tired, uncomfortable, and under pressure. "What battle am I losing?" is more useful than "Why am I bad from here?" "Where is my elbow?" is more useful than panic. "Can I make them face the wrong way?" gives shape to a position that otherwise feels like a scramble. "Where are they heavy and where are they light" is a better answer than just giving in when you're being smashed in side control and can't escape.

Teaching made this more clear. A student can copy a movement and still not know why it works. Another student can miss the exact movement but understand the problem well enough to improvise. The second student usually becomes proficient more quickly, because they are not waiting for a perfect memory; they are reading the reasons behind the position.

## Why Concepts Matter

A technique works because of the concept underneath it. If I teach an armbar from mount, the technical steps matter: position of the knees, control of the arm, angle of the hips, protection against escapes, isolation mechanics, and finishing mechanics. But the lesson underneath those steps is more general. Separate the elbow from the ribs, and climb until the defender's arms cannot stay connected to their posture. Use a large part of your body against a smaller part of theirs. Create a pin before attempting a break, and force their escape route to be a late-stage escape.

That same lesson appears in other places. In side control you separate elbows from ribs; on the back, hands from the neck; in leg entanglements, the knee line from its freedom.

Concepts also help students survive bad moments. A beginner stuck in bottom side control may not remember the exact steps of the basic white belt escape. They can still ask better questions. Am I flat? Can I turn to a side? Do I have an inside frame? Is my elbow connected to my knee? Is my spine helping me move, or am I twisted into the mat? Where is my opponent heavy? Where are they light? Those questions do not replace coaching or learning the technique of the escape, but they give the student something useful while under pressure if they don't know the escape or the escape isn't working.

## What This Book Is Not

No book can be a technique encyclopedia, complete curriculum, or replacement for live training. Jiu jitsu has to be felt. Pressure, timing, weight distribution, and resistance cannot be fully learned from a page. Neither can fear, fatigue, failure, or success.

The purpose here is narrower and, I hope, more durable. I want to give you a way to look at positions. If I had to reduce the book to one short paragraph, it would be this: fundamentals are not beginner techniques. They are the recurring concepts that let any practitioner diagnose a position, adapt when it changes, and keep learning long after the move of the week is forgotten. Everything else in this book is an attempt to make that usable under pressure.

The chapters that follow return to a handful of recurring ideas: head height, hip height, grips, posture and base, quadrants, and Konami Code (reference to late 1980s Nintendo Entertainment System games). They also return to centerline, inside position, strike zone, and two-on-one control, along with isolation, leverage, spinal alignment, and Newton's Third Law: every action has an equal and opposite reaction. You will see those ideas in guard, passing, and pins, and again in submissions, escapes, back control, and leg entanglements. Repetition is intentional. Jiu jitsu starts to make more sense when some of the same questions (and some of the same answers) follow you from position to position.

This book is not meant to be exhaustive in its examination of positions, offensive maneuvers, or defensive maneuvers. Certain topics are covered in a more coarse manner, while others are covered in a fine-grained manner, and you will certainly find topics that are not even touched. The intent is to cover a material portion of what you encounter in a way that causes you to reframe the way you think about positions both offensively and defensively.

If you're looking for a detailed breakdown of a specific position under a certain configuration and certain set of circumstances, this is not the book for you.

If you're looking for a way to mentally model how to think through positions in general, using very specific examples to illustrate general concepts and near-global truths, this book is for you.

## How to Read This Book

Read it slowly. The chapters are intentionally small. Mentally take one to training and watch for it. Do not try to recognize every idea in every exchange; this book is not a checklist. Feel free to jump around to specific topics for areas where you need to supplement your knowledge. Pick one or two items that stood out while reading and search for one at a time. You may go looking for centerline opportunities and suddenly remember a concept about knee-elbow connections instead. You may go looking for posture and realize the problem was grips. That is still a good result.

Highlight the book heavily if that helps you. Write in the margins. Keep a small training journal. Mark the phrases you want to notice, experiment with, or revisit. After a round, write one sentence: "I lost side control bottom because my inside elbow was gone," or "The pass worked when I pinned the hips before chasing the head." A sentence like that is more valuable than a long list of techniques you half-remember, because it tells you what to study next.

My hope is that you do not need to read this book twice in the normal way. I hope it becomes a pointer. You read a section, bring one observation to the mat, test it, make a note, then come back when the next problem becomes visible.

If you are a coach, treat the language in this book as raw material. Keep what fits your room. Change what does not sound like you. The best coaching language is short enough to reach a student under pressure and precise enough to change them for the better.

Different ranks need different things from the same concepts. The same idea lands differently for a white belt, a purple belt, a coach, a wrestler new to jiu jitsu, a hobbyist, or a competitor, which is what the next chapter takes up.
