# Intended Audience

Fundamentals are often misunderstood because people treat the word as a synonym for beginner material. Traditionally, fundamentals means knowing the core positions, submissions, defenses, sweeps, and techniques. To me that is not true, or at least not complete.

The main reader for this book is the student looking for a more cerebral approach to jiu jitsu, not just a rolodex of techniques or more moves to memorize. They want to understand the why behind the positions, the attacks, the defenses, and the decisions that appear everywhere.

That student may not be stuck on anything in particular. They may already train well and just want a better mental model. Or they may be lost in the more familiar sea of "I do not know what I am doing." Either way, the goal is to give them a way to think about and approach jiu jitsu through foundational concepts that apply everywhere and in every position.

Fundamentals mean understanding each position well enough to be competent inside it. You can navigate toward advancement, attack, submission, and control. You can be comfortable when challenged, under pressure, or under attack because you understand the threats, know how to keep yourself safe, know how to improve your position, and can sometimes create attack opportunities of your own. A concept is fundamental because every part of the game keeps returning to it, whether or not it is easy.

Said another way, fundamentals are not beginner techniques. They are the recurring concepts that let any practitioner diagnose a position, adapt when it changes, and keep learning after the move of the week is forgotten. That definition holds as you improve, because it is the same job for a white belt trying to survive side control and a black belt deciding which submission to hunt from it.

## Newer Students

Newer students should use this book as a set of landmarks. In the beginning, jiu jitsu can feel like being dropped into a city without street names. Thankfully, there are only a handful of core jiu jitsu positions to understand, and this book assumes you have a basic awareness of guard, side control, mount, and knee on belly, to name a few.

Most newer students find that every round has too much information. Someone passes, mounts, takes the back, attacks the neck, and the student walks away with only a blur of what happened and potentially a lot of discomfort.

Landmarks help. If you can notice that your head is lower, your hips are pinned, your elbow has crossed your sternum, or your spine is twisted away from your hips, the position is no longer just "bad." It has a structure, which gives you a defined problem to study.

Do not try to master every concept at once. Pick one. Spend a week watching elbows, then another watching head height. During side control rounds, ask whether you are flat or on a side. In guard, ask whether your grips create control or only contact, and check if every limb has a job or not at any given moment. Progress often begins when you stop asking for the perfect move and start seeing the first battle you lost.

## Intermediate Students

Blue and purple belts often have the opposite problem. They know enough techniques to become scattered. One month is half guard, the next is leg locks, then passing, then back attacks, and the game expands faster than the structure holding it together.

Conceptual study helps intermediate students connect what they already know. A knee-cut pass, a body-lock pass, and a torreando pass are different movements, but all of them ask questions about hip control, frames, centerline, and base. Submissions work the same way: whether you finish with a triangle, an armbar, or a back choke, you depend on separation, isolation, immobilization, and the finish. On defense, guard management (I loathe calling it guard retention), mount escape, and side control recovery all ask you to rebuild alignment and inside position.

Once those links become visible, training becomes less random. You still learn techniques, but you stop treating each new technique as a separate island.

## Advanced Students and Coaches

Experienced practitioners may not need this book to tell them that posture matters. They already know. The book can sharpen the words around what they know and make the invisible parts easier to teach. I like to think that what we know subconsciously gets sharper once we make it conscious.

A long explanation may be useful before or after class, but during a round the student needs a phrase that changes behavior now.

Some of the language I return to is simple:

- Four things win battles: head height, hip height, grips, and posture and base.
- If your limb is not doing something, do something with it.
- Grapplers who use their whole body grow faster than their peers.

The next four ideas come from Chris Haueter's well-known four rules of grappling. I did not invent them, but I come back to them often because they keep students honest. If you're not familiar with Chris Haueter, he is one of the "dirty dozen". The dirty dozen were the first dozen non-Brazilian practitioners promoted to black belt. Jiu jitsu can become complicated fast, and Chris's four rules pull the student back toward priorities. They are:

1. Be the person on top.
1. When you are on top, stay on top.
1. When you are on bottom, have a guard they shall not pass.
1. Never forget the first rule.

Advanced students can also use the book as a diagnostic. When a position stops working, the answer is not always a new move. Sometimes the old move has a slightly different condition or dependency than what is provided by your current position. Your passing may fail because you stopped controlling hips before chasing head control, your submissions because you are attacking before isolation, and your escapes because you are trying to move with a misaligned spine.

## Grapplers From Other Arts

Wrestlers, judoka, sambo players, MMA athletes, and other grapplers will recognize most of the ideas here. Head position, hip position, pressure, and grips belong to grappling as a whole, and so do balance, angles, posts, and pins.

A wrestler may have excellent pressure but overextend an arm inside closed guard. A judoka who understands off-balancing may need to adjust to prolonged ground control, coming from a sport that provides generally less encouragement for ground fighting. An MMA athlete may already respect posture and head position because strikes punish mistakes quickly, but sport jiu jitsu may ask for patience in positions that would be unacceptable under strikes.
