# What Wins Battles

Most positions are won before the technique gets a name.

People usually describe the visible ending. They say the pass worked, the sweep worked, the escape failed, or the submission came on fast. That language is convenient, but it hides the earlier losses. Someone's head slipped to the wrong side, or someone's hips were pinned. A grip became control instead of contact. Posture broke, or the opponent's base (we will discuss what base is in a moment) stretched too far in one direction.

The first diagnostic question in this book is "What battle am I losing?"

The four things I return to most often are head height, hip height, grips, and posture and base. A lost head position can break posture, and broken posture can weaken base. A grip can pin the hips, while hip movement can restore posture. Jiu jitsu feels complicated because these battles overlap, but the overlap is also what makes the art readable. When you do not know what technique to use, you can still look for the battle.

Closed guard illustrates this well. A student can know the scissor sweep and still fail if they only memorize the leg motion. If the sleeve-side post is only loosely controlled, the post is still available to the top player. If the sleeve-side post is controlled but the top player posts with the knee, the sweep may be stopped. The technique failed because the bottom player tried to move through a defended quadrant, not because scissor sweeps are fake. Once the bottom player broke posture and changed the direction of the sweep, the same family of movement became useful again.

## Head Height

Head position affects direction. It influences balance, pressure, breathing, visibility, and the ability to turn. In many exchanges, the player who controls head position decides where the next movement is allowed to go.

Passing gives an easy example. As the passer works to open the guard (separating the feet) and clear the legs, the head is one of the first things being fought over. A passer who keeps the head up, on the correct side, and hard to reach gives the guard player nothing to grab, and makes it harder for them to sit up, turn in, or recover an angle. A passer who drops the head carelessly, or lets it drift to the wrong side, hands that same head over: the guard player can snap it down, redirect it, push it away, climb over it, guillotine it, or use it to break back to posture.

Wrestling exchanges show this more obviously. A good head position can open the hips and legs, while a poor one can leave the neck exposed, the spine bent forward, or the body reaching without structure. From bottom pins, the defender's head position often decides whether they can turn to a side or be forced to remain flat. From back control, a few inches of head position can hide a choking line or give it away.

"Head height" as a phrase is useful coaching language only when the student knows what the head is winning. Putting your head somewhere wins nothing by itself. You win with head position by using it to give direction, block rotation, connect to your spine, or stop the opponent from aligning their body.

## Hip Height

Hip position determines how weight and mobility behave. High hips can create pressure, speed, and passing angles, but they can also become light, exposed, or easy to tip. Low hips can create stability and weight, and they can also become stuck.

Guard passing is a constant argument over hip mobility. The passer wants to pin the opponent's shoulders to the mat, staple parts of their body such as their legs to the mat (downward pressure to prevent movement), redirect their limbs, or step around the guard player's hips. The guard player wants their own hips free enough to angle, invert, sit up, recover, or off-balance. A passer who controls the opponent's feet but never controls the hips may look busy while the guard player keeps re-facing them. A guard player with beautiful grips but dead hips may still be passed.

Half guard comes down to hip height. From the bottom, once I win the underhook and have an opportunity to escape my head, the position turns into a contest over whose hips are higher. The instant I lift my hips above my opponent's, I am no longer only escaping; I can come up and take top position or threaten the back. The same battle catches the top player by surprise, because being on top is not the same as winning the position. If the bottom player has worked their hips up from underneath, even while holding the half guard leg entanglement, then the bottom player's hips are higher and the position is theirs, no matter who looks like they are on top.

Head height and hip height are how you satisfy the first two of Chris Haueter's rules of grappling: be the guy on top, and when on top, stay on top.

"Hip height" as a coaching phrase during a competitive round is useful when the practitioner understands this concept. It should be an indicator to begin working to top position by adjusting your body's position to elevate hips above the opponent.

## Grips

Cloth, wrists, ankles, collars, and underhooks (just about anything to which you establish connection) can be used as a grip. A grip is not control just because the hand is closed. Grips only matter when they prevent something, like movement or defense, or create something useful such as a submission or escape opportunity. A collar grip that does not break posture, create leverage, make space, or set up a submission does nothing for you, and a sleeve grip that cannot stop a post is temporary contact. An underhook that does not give access to the back or establish hip control is incomplete.

Good grips can and do change the opponent's choices. Grips make the opponent carry weight, deny their post, expose an elbow, turn a shoulder, or delay a recovery. Sometimes the best grip is not the strongest grip but the one that establishes the next battle, increases or decreases opportunity space (what someone can or cannot do next), or makes the next battle easier.

The gi makes this lesson obvious because there are so many things to hold that do not provide any form of control. A student may feel secure because they have a sleeve or collar, but the opponent is still postured, balanced, and free to move. A weak grip is easily defeated or countered. No-gi exposes this problem faster, as there is nothing to connect to other than your opponent's body. Poor grips either get exposed or disappear, and the student learns whether they had control or only attachment.

"Grips" as a coaching phrase is useful in a variety of situations. If the person I'm coaching has a limb that isn't doing anything, it's an indication to give it a job. If their opponent has dominant grips on them, it's an indication to fight the grip to regain the dominant grip.

## Posture and Base

Posture and base are linked closely enough that I often treat them as one. Posture is useful structure under pressure. Base is the ability to resist unwanted movement, and one without the other is incomplete.

A player with a useful body structure that can tolerate pressure (posture) can apply force without collapsing, defend without overextending, move without falling, and keep enough space to allow for chest expansion. Being able to breathe well reduces stress and anxiety, so the player is better prepared to make rapid decisions. The exact structure changes by position. In closed guard, posture may mean spine, hips, elbows, and hands are organized well enough to open the guard safely. Under side control, it may mean frames, head position, and spinal alignment are organized well enough to turn in to face your opponent and maintain the position. During a leg entanglement, posture may mean the defender can stand, clear the knee line, and avoid dangerous rotation.

Students often mistake stiffness for posture. Stiffness can feel strong for a moment, particularly during drilling, but a stiff body is easy to redirect. Useful posture has connection without rigidity, letting the player adjust as the problem changes.

Posture and base also belong to both players. In closed guard, the top player is trying to keep posture and the bottom player is trying to break it. The passer wants posture while entering, and the guard player wants it broken or at minimum controlled before attacking or sweeping. Broken posture generally means a compromised skeletal structure, which could be a folded spine, a twist, or an unnatural alignment of any kind. In almost every position, each player is attempting to build and establish structure, and battles are often won based on who can build and establish that structure faster.

Base is not just having knees wide or hands on the mat. A good base counters the threat that exists right now, and threats change. A wide base may stop lateral movement and still be vulnerable to being pulled forward, while a staggered base may stop a push and expose rotation of the hips or a limb. A hand post may save a sweep and expose an arm. A knee may block a hip and still leave the upper body easy to turn.

Sweeps make base easy to study, because a sweep may look like one motion when the base was usually compromised first. The guard player does not need to lift the entire opponent if the opponent's post has been removed, trapped, shortened, redirected, or overloaded, and a fulcrum over which the opponent can be moved is established. A sleeve grip can stop the hand from posting, and a hook such as your own in-step (the top part of your foot) can elevate your opponent's leg so their foot no longer helps. A pull can move weight beyond the point where the opponent can recover.

Base also has a psychological component. Tired students often post anywhere that feels safe. Experienced opponents turn those emergency posts into handles. A tired student just wants to stop falling. The more useful aim is to fall in a way that doesn't give away the next problem.

## What Winning a Battle Opens Up

What winning a battle gets you is hard to state as a general rule but easy to show on the mat, so here are four demonstrations.

Start with head height. Picture the bottom player in half guard coming up to the underhook. The underhook alone does not win this; head height does. Once the head clears the underhook and comes up, the bottom player is no longer pinned to the mat. They are mobile and can start driving forward or downward pressure into the person who had been on top, and that pressure is what turns a defensive position into a dominant one.

Hip height pays off differently. When the hips come up in half guard after clearing the head using the underhook, the bottom player regains leg mobility, which can go looking for work (like establishing back control with hooks) and, just as important, the ability to create driving pressure with the hips. You raise the hips to give the legs freedom to go somewhere. A player with free hips can start entangling the opponent's strike zone with the legs (covered in the Strike Zone chapter), threading a limb into a place that a moment earlier held only weight.

Grips flip the workload. Win the grip fight and the opponent now has to deal with your grips while you move on to whatever you actually wanted. The clearest version is a submission. I cannot start an effective kimura without a good grip on the wrist or real control of the deltoid. Win the grip first and the attack has something to stand on. Skip it and the attack is a wish. In almost any scenario you can conjure, the person that establishes the dominant grips will be dictating where the fight takes place next.

Posture and base pay off less visibly: winning them makes it more difficult to lose position and easier to advance it. When your posture and base are solid, the opponent has a harder time compromising your position, and you are freer to focus on the thing you came to do. Most of the time they have to break your posture or base before they can run their own offense at all, so holding those two is often what keeps their plan from ever starting.

## The Battle You Neglect

People sometimes ask which of the four battles is the most important, or which one students get wrong the most often. Almost everyone neglects one or more of these battles, but not the same one. One student fights grips beautifully and forgets their head is being driven to the mat, while another keeps perfect posture and never notices their hips are stuck underneath them. A third has heavy, mobile hips and grips that never do a real job. The neglected battle is personal.

This chapter is a diagnostic, not a ranking. Do not decide in advance that head height is your weakness because a book said it was common. Let the mat tell you. When a position falls apart, rewind it and ask which of the four you stopped paying attention to. Over a few weeks a pattern usually appears, and it is often the same battle failing in guard, in passing, and in escapes, because a blind spot travels with you. Find your own neglected battle before you go looking for a new technique to cover it.

If you coach, the same rule holds in reverse. Resist handing every student the same correction. The student who needs "get your head off the mat" and the student who needs "make that grip do something" are losing different battles, and one cue will fail half the room. Watch first, name the neglected battle for that person, then give them one thing to fix.

## Training Observation

During one round, ignore the names of techniques. When something works or fails, rewind the exchange in your mind and name the first battle that changed. Head height, hip height, grips, or posture and base? A student who can identify the first lost battle learns faster than a student who only remembers the final score.

The next chapter adds direction to those battles. If posture and base decides whether someone can resist movement, quadrants help explain where that movement can go.
