# Quadrants

Sweeps become easier to understand when you stop thinking only in terms of left and right.

The top player's base is distributed across eight useful quadrants: up, down, left, and right, plus the four diagonals of upper left, upper right, lower left, and lower right. Hands, knees, feet, hips, head position, and grips can all protect one of those quadrants. When one quadrant is blocked, posted, or too heavy to move through, you do not always have to force harder, because you may be able to move the opponent through a different quadrant. Advanced practitioners are effective at breaking the eight quadrants down even further.

Quadrants give the bottom player a map for direction. They also give the top player a way to understand why a sweep that seemed defended may still be available somewhere else.

## The Scissor Sweep Problem

In a closed-guard class, we were working through the mindset of the person on top and the person on bottom. The technique in focus was a basic scissor sweep. The bottom player had a collar-sleeve grip. The top player's left arm was controlled, which meant the obvious hand post on that side was eliminated. Most students would look at that and think the sweep should work because the post was gone and the direction was available.

The person on top found another answer. They extended their left knee outward and effectively posted with it. The arm post was gone, but the lower-left quadrant was still protected. The scissor sweep stalled because the bottom player was trying to move the opponent through the quadrant the top player had just reinforced with the knee.

Without abandoning the position, the bottom player broke posture, loaded the top player's weight onto their hips (pulling them forward), and redirected the sweep toward the upper right quadrant, which bypassed the knee post entirely. The top player still had the knee post, but it stopped being effective once they were no longer being moved in that direction.

## Posts Protect Quadrants

A post is anything that stops movement, whether that is a hand, a foot, a knee, a head, or a grip. In the scissor-sweep example, the top player's knee did the job most students expect the hand to do.

A sweep fails when the opponent can place enough structure into the quadrant you are trying to move them into. For it to succeed, that quadrant has to have no post, or a post that is overloaded or shortened, or a post that is no longer relevant because you changed the direction.

Quadrants tie directly to the earlier battle over Posture and Base (refer back to the What Wins Battles chapter), because posture and base point somewhere. A player may have strong base in one direction and weak base in another: impossible to move toward the posted knee yet easy to move toward the loaded shoulder, or able to stop the first sweep and still late for the second direction.

Instead of asking, "Why did my sweep fail?", ask, "Which quadrant did I try to move them through, and what post did they use to save it?"

## Loading Weight

Quadrants are about loading weight as much as they are about removing posts. If the opponent's weight is still centered and their posture is intact, every quadrant may feel defended. When posture breaks, the head drops and the spine bends forward, and the hips become lighter or heavier depending on the angle, so the map changes. Limbs that are still available no longer connect to the floor as usefully.

In closed guard, pulling the top player forward and breaking their posture can load their weight onto your hips. Once you are carrying their weight instead of their own base cleanly carrying it, a direction that was unavailable may open, and what was a sideways shove against a post becomes a rotation over a loaded point.

That language connects quadrants to fulcrums and levers, the subject of the Fulcrums and Levers chapter. The hip, shin, calf, or foot can become the structure the opponent moves over, just the same as it could be your hand, forearm, elbow, head, or torso. The sleeve grip may remove one post while the collar grip breaks posture, and the leg position may block the knee or create the ramp. The quadrant you choose is where the weight needs to go.

## The Top Player's Job

The top player should not think, "I stopped the sweep, so I am safe." They should ask what they used to stop it and what using it exposed. A good post solves one direction without giving away too much in another, whereas a bad post stops the first sweep and allows the opponent to start the next attack.

If the top player posts a hand, the hand may become a sleeve grip, arm drag, kimura, or triangle problem. A posted knee may make the upper-body quadrants easier to move through. Extending the base backward to avoid being swept forward may let the bottom player gain head and hip height, sit up, or begin a different attack, and when the top player postures hard, the bottom player may follow them into a hip bump, guillotine, kimura, or triangle threat.

## The Bottom Player's Job

The bottom player should learn to feel the blocked quadrant without becoming stubborn. If the sweep stops, do not automatically squeeze harder, kick harder, or blame the technique. Ask where the top player saved themselves. Bruce Lee famously said "Be like water", and in that way, be malleable enough to adjust the sweep.

Did they post a hand or widen a knee? Did they shift the hips back, raise the head, turn the spine, or hide the elbow? Each answer points toward the next attack.

Quadrants belong next to dilemmas (covered in the Dilemmas chapter). A dilemma forces the opponent to answer one threat in a way that exposes another. Quadrants help you see the direction of that exposure. The opponent saves the lower-left quadrant with a knee, so you load them and move them through the upper right. They posture to reclaim one line, so you follow them into the space they created.

## Training Observation

Pick one sweep and study only direction. Do not ask whether the sweep "works." Ask which quadrant you are trying to move the opponent through, then which post saves that quadrant.

In closed guard, start with a scissor-sweep style position. Let the top player defend with the hand, knee, hip, or posture. The bottom player does not get to force the same direction twice. They must name the post, change the loading, and move the opponent through a different quadrant.

The next chapter widens the directional map with a coaching shorthand borrowed from old video games, the Konami Code. Quadrants tell you where the opponent can be moved, and the Konami Code points to which other directions are still available when the first one is defended.
