# Konami Code

The Konami Code is a useful way to remind students that a position usually has more directions than the one they are currently forcing.

The name comes from video games produced by a company called Konami in the late 1980s. I played these non-stop on my trusty old Nintendo Entertainment System (NES). Yes, the one that demanded you blow the dust out of the cartridges and the cartridge slots. I did mention that I'm old, right?

In games like Contra, the famous cheat code moved through directions and buttons: ↑, ↑, ↓, ↓, ←, →, ←, →, B, A, start. Executed successfully, you start with more lives, or have a full complement of weapons available to you. On the mat, the code becomes a question students can remember: what options exist up, down, left, and right from here, and what exists backward, forward, and diagonally?

Students often fail by becoming single-directional. They try to sweep only to the right, escape only by pushing away, or pass only forward. These patterns emerge as habits and are reinforced by success. Once the opponent blocks that pattern, the student usually either forces harder or assumes the position is dead. This is relevant to both offensive and defensive positions. It's usually not completely dead, but an indicator that other options might exist in different directions and should be considered.

## Directions Are Options

The earlier chapter on quadrants gives a map for sweeping and base. The Konami Code widens the map. Instead of seeing only the defended quadrant, ask what each direction offers.

Up could mean:

- building height
- attacking the outside arm
- coming to a seated position
- forcing the opponent to post

Down could mean:

- dropping under the hips
- entering deep half
- pulling the opponent's weight over a new fulcrum

Left might mean:

- head-side attacks
- collar attacks
- angles toward the neck

Right might mean:

- hip-side movement
- underhook entries
- deep-half pathways

Diagonal movement is often where the openings are, because positions rarely move in clean compass points.

Stop treating one blocked path as the end of the exchange and find alternate routes to your desired destination, without becoming cute with directions.

## Bottom Half Guard

Bottom half guard is the number one place where I teach the Konami Code. Half guard is my favorite position because it exemplifies jiu jitsu. It's dynamic. The top player doesn't hold all the cards. The bottom player actually holds more but is disadvantaged by position and gravity, and that disadvantage is why the Konami Code is so useful there.

Let's start with what I would consider a "good" bottom half-guard position. The shoulders should be vertical rather than flat, that is, a perpendicular line between the alignment of your shoulders and the mat. With vertical shoulders, one shoulder is fully off the mat and in a straight line from your bottom shoulder to the ceiling. You can crunch in or extend away with ease. Your bottom leg entangles the near side leg and makes a measured connection, with degrees of force that you control. Your hands and elbows are active with either grips or frames. Vertical shoulders make it difficult for your opponent to flatten you. The leg entanglement gives you control of the top player's mobility and options for how you begin your offensive or defensive motion, and the frames and grips allow you to execute your plan.

From that position, specifically with vertical shoulders, the Konami Code becomes practical. Up is the outside arm: kimuras, sit-up sweeps, shoulder control, or attacks that punish the top player for posting carelessly. To the left, on the head and collar side, you have choke opportunities, head control, collar access, and attacks that make the top player protect the neck. Down is the underhook side, so you can come underneath, build to the knees, wrestle up, or force the top player to defend the lower-body connection. Going right takes you to the deep-half side, where you get under the hips, change the weight distribution, and make the top player's passing pressure less direct.

Directional thinking is what makes half guard feel so rewarding from bottom position. It is certainly why I consider it my hub and a position I force rather than chasing submissions from guard. The top player often sees one main job: pass. The bottom player sees directions: the far arm, through the collar and sleeve, to the underhook, or down to deep half, along with chances to wrestle up, sweep, recover, or escape. Even when the exact techniques change, the mental model keeps the bottom player asking useful questions, and the Konami Code is a quick reminder to examine opportunities in all directions.

## Direction Requires Posture

The Konami Code only helps if the position is alive enough to support choices. A flat bottom half guard with the shoulders pinned to the mat does not offer the same directional options as a half guard position where one of your shoulders is free and clear of the mat, and preferably in a vertical orientation. A seated open guard has more wrestling options with a backstop arm than it has once it collapses and the head is controlled. Closed guard works the same way, since breaking posture and establishing grips gives you different options than you have lying flat and disconnected.

Posture decides how many directions are available and feasible. Head height, hip height, grips, and posture and base determine which ones you can use. A student who has no strike-zone protection, and no useful elbow position, may technically know several options but cannot reach them. A student with good posture and useful limbs may not need a large movement; a small directional change can open the next attack.

Half guard also shows why the idea carries into worse positions. Side control, mount, and back control still have directions, but the defender may have to earn them back before they are usable. Recovering a frame, protecting an elbow, clearing head pressure, getting off of your back and onto your side, or winning a hand fight can be the difference between a real direction and a fantasy.

## Forcing Versus Reading

The Konami Code is not permission to spam options. Directional thinking should make the student more observant.

A student who understands its job uses the idea as an opportunity scan. Evaluate every direction, knowing that not every direction will be available, and when one is blocked, look elsewhere instead of forcing harder into the same wall. "Be like water".

Observe what your opponent is defending, then ask which direction their defense just opened. If they block the right-side sweep with a knee post, the upper-right quadrant may still be available after posture is broken. In half guard, a shut-down underhook may leave the outside-arm attack available. When they posture away from a collar-sleeve threat in closed guard, follow them into the space they create, and if they drive forward in open guard, look for seated wrestling or shin-to-shin.

Sometimes you set up a direction you do not want: if the opponent takes the bait and over-defends it, they may give you the direction you wanted the whole time. Besides recovering from a blocked path, you can use the Konami Code to build reactions on purpose and start an offensive or defensive cycle.

## Training Observation

Start in bottom half guard with vertical shoulders, that is, with your deltoids in a vertical line from the mat to the ceiling. The bottom player is not allowed to repeat the same direction twice. They must try one attack or recovery in each direction. The top player gives resistance and names what they had to defend, but does not try to advance.

After the round, ask which directions were usable and which were not. A direction is real when the player's posture, grips, inside position, and connection support it, and fake when it exists only as an idea while the body is pinned. This drill benefits both the bottom and top person in that the bottom person has to evaluate options in each direction, and the top person has to respond and see what opened up as a result of that response.

The next chapter turns to the centerline. Direction gives you options, but elbow position and body orientation decide whether those options are yours or belong to the opponent.
