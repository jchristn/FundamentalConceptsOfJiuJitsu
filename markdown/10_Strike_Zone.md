# Strike Zone

I learned about the strike zone (the area between the armpit and the hip) and many other concepts from Andrew Kerfoot at Nexus Jiu Jitsu in Folsom, California, and understanding this space conceptually has fundamentally changed the way I look at jiu jitsu.

If your opponent controls the strike zone, they decide where you can and cannot go. They can hold your shoulder while controlling your hip, create space between your elbow and your midsection, or prevent your knee from reconnecting. They can flatten you, turn you, climb you, pass you, or expose your back.

Good elbow position protects the strike zone, which is where centerline and inside position do their work. An elbow on the correct side of the body can guard the lane between armpit and hip. When it gets dragged across the sternum, the lane opens, and the opponent can take the clear path without doing anything complicated.

## Why the Space Decides So Much

The area between the armpit and the hip connects the upper body to the lower body and is generally made up of lats, ribs, and obliques. Control that area and you can begin separating the person into pieces. You can turn the shoulder while the hip is pinned, or flatten the upper body while the hip is blocked, and you can lift the elbow while keeping the knee from reconnecting. However strong the defender is, that strength no longer travels cleanly through the whole body.

Side control makes the idea obvious. Side control only holds from top if the top player controls the near-side strike zone. That control gives the top player influence over the bottom player's hips, which lets them limit movement while creating chances to advance and submit. The top player pairs strike-zone control with control of the head, the underhook, and the hips, along with the pressure necessary to slow or stall the bottom player's movement.

If the top player owns the strike zone, the bottom player is usually late. The bottom player's job is to recover the strike zone, or begin an offensive process, which also requires recovering the strike-zone space. Frames put elbows and forearms between the bottom player's body and the top player's body, so the bottom player can make space and then take it. A good underhook allows the bottom player to turn space management into strike-zone control against the top player. Once the bottom player guards that armpit-to-hip lane or steals it back, or begins controlling it on the top player, wrestling up, escaping out the back door, sweeping, reversing, or attacking all become more realistic.

Mount shows the same problem from a different angle. The top player climbs when the elbows separate from the ribs, either willingly or by force, and the climb ends in what is known as a "high mount." High mount is the result of controlling the space between the defender's elbows, armpits, chest, and hips, which makes it a form of strike-zone control from top position. The bottom player survives by keeping elbows useful, protecting the neck, stopping the top player from climbing into that lane, and using limbs to try to reclaim that space.

## Back Exposure

The strike zone also explains why back exposure appears before the back is fully taken. When the opponent controls the space between armpit and hip, they can start turning you away from them. Your limbs begin facing the wrong direction and your eyes face away from the person attacking you. Because you cannot see what they are doing, you have to react rather than lead, and that disadvantage is massive.

## Offensive Use

For the attacker, the strike zone is a target. In passing, that means controlling the hips and winning the inside space near the armpit. From side control you deny the near-side frame and far-side underhook; from mount you climb through the elbows. Back attacks call for controlling the shoulder line while winning the hand fight. In closed guard bottom, pull posture, control the sleeve or collar, and make the opponent's elbows unreliable.

Do not grab randomly at the body. Each point of control should have a purpose, and the strike zone helps you identify which ones are worth taking. Ask which part lets them turn, which part lets them frame, which part lets them recover a knee, and which part lets them see and face the problem.

## Defensive Use

For the defender, the first job is usually to deny clean ownership of that lane, and if the attacker owns it, get control of it back. Keep elbows on the correct side of the body, reconnect elbow to knee, and build inside frames. Win the hand fight before the neck is exposed, and keep the hip from being pinned long enough to turn, recover guard, or come up.

A defender needs useful position to survive, not perfect position. A single inside elbow can be enough to keep the exchange alive, and so can one knee line, one frame, one hip angle, or one hand inside the choking arm. Lose every useful structure in the strike zone and the position starts to become a pin, then a pass, then a submission process. Entire side guard systems have been built on recovering an inside knee-elbow connection from bottom side control.

## Training Observation

Start in side control, mount, or seated open guard. The observing partner names the strike zone on the defender: armpit to hip. The attacking partner tries to own that space without rushing a submission. The defender tries to keep at least one elbow, knee, frame, or hip angle useful inside that lane.

After each reset, ask one question: who controlled the armpit-to-hip space, and what did that control allow next?

The next chapter turns from controlling the lane to controlling a limb. Once centerline, inside position, and strike-zone control are connected, a two-on-one can make one of the opponent's arms unreliable enough to expose the back, remove a post, or start the isolation process.
