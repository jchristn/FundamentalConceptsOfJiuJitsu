# Process of Submission

I constantly remind my students that the process of submission is systematically cutting your opponent in half such that you have a consequential part of your body attacking an inconsequential part of your opponent, for instance, your entire body attacking an arm or the neck.

Submissions are often taught as final positions: the armbar, triangle, rear naked choke, and heel hook, along with the kimura, guillotine, wrist lock, and many others. The grips, body orientation, and configuration of the limbs are essential details in properly executing a submission. Often these details have the side effect of distracting students from the process that made the finish possible.

A clean submission begins long before the tap, generally when the defender has been cut in half, lost grips, or has their posture compromised. The attacker keeps reducing the defender's ability to move as a whole until a large part of the attacker's body is aimed at a smaller and more vulnerable part of the defender's body.

Exert control, then cut your opponent in half. Pass closed guard into half guard. Exert control, then cut your opponent in half again. Move from half guard to side control. Exert control, then cut your opponent in half again. Move from side control through knee on belly to mount. Each position on that path describes how much of the opponent is still available to defend as a whole.

In most guards, it is body against body. Side control starts to make it your body against half of theirs, and in mount it becomes your body against their chest, neck, arms, and shoulders. By the time a good submission appears, the defender should feel like one isolated part of them is being asked to solve a problem created by far more of you.

## Isolation and Control Before Break

Each submission should begin as a pin, unless you are a fan of "ripping" submissions (I'm not). Before the armbar is an armbar, the arm is pinned in a way that makes escape from the position difficult by pulling the arm into your chest and essentially hyperextending the bicep, then clamping down with your thighs and applying downward force on the face and hips with your hamstrings and calves. Before the choke is a choke, the shoulders, head, defensive hands, and posture are being pinned into worse and worse positions. Before a leg lock is a leg lock, the hip and knee line are controlled well enough that the defender cannot turn out, kick free, or reconnect. The submission begins when the endangered part can no longer return easily to the rest of the body before an attack completes.

In an armbar, the arm is not truly isolated unless the elbow is separated from the ribs, the shoulder line is controlled, the bicep virtually hyperextended, the head posture managed, and the defender's hands prevented from rebuilding a strong connection. A triangle separates the head and arm from the rest of the body while posture is broken and angle is created. The neck is exposed for a rear naked choke only after the attacker has managed shoulders, hands, head position, and hip control; in a heel hook, rotational force should not even be the focus until the knee line and hip relationship have been controlled.

Although the details change by submission, the process repeats. Find the part of the body that matters in the offensive sequence you are executing, then separate and isolate it. Keep it separated and control it. Use your body in a way the defender cannot answer with the isolated part alone.

In top side control with both arms on the far side of the bottom player's body, attempting a straight arm lock, kimura, or americana from there starts with the arm pinned away from safety, the shoulder controlled, and the defender's normal response delayed. The finish comes last. If the bottom player turns away from the top player to pull the trapped arm back, the top player may be able to attack the exposed back as well as the arm.

Students rush because submissions are exciting. They see the arm, neck, or foot and jump to the ending. The defender is still whole enough to posture, turn, stack, and hand fight, and still free to pummel, hide the elbow, clear the knee line, or move the hips. The attack fails because the defender still had too many connections, not because the student did not squeeze hard enough.

## Large Against Small

Jiu jitsu becomes efficient when you use a large part of your body against a smaller part of the opponent's body, as when the hips work against an elbow, the legs and torso against a shoulder, or the chest and arms against the neck. A whole-body angle can do the same to a wrist, knee, or ankle. The more of you that attacks the less of them, the less force you need.

Strength, athleticism, and size all still matter. Pretending otherwise makes the art sound dishonest. Leverage organizes the body so more of its strength reaches the target. A smaller person can create real power when their hips, spine, grips, and legs work together against an isolated joint or trapped line. A larger person can become surprisingly weak when the attacked part has been separated from the rest of their structure or their spine misaligned.

The battles in this book all feed this process by narrowing the fight to a smaller target. Head height can break posture, centerline control can pull an elbow away from safety, and strike-zone control can separate the upper body from the hips. A two-on-one can make one limb useless as a post or frame, hip control can stop the defender from turning, and spinal misalignment can make a strong person unable to bridge or stack (the Spinal Alignment chapter covers this).

## The Defender's Reintegration

The defender's job is usually to reconnect, and most of that reconnection reduces to three jobs: protect the target (hide the elbow, bring the hands back together, tuck the endangered limb back into the whole body), rebuild structure (recover inside frames, rebuild posture, square the shoulders), and restore direction (clear the knee line, turn the hips, face the head and spine somewhere useful).

The attacker's job runs separate, isolate, immobilize, finish, while the defender's runs reconnect, align, recover movement, escape. The two sides are mirror images of one another, in reverse directions. The lists are short, but the live exchange is not, and good jiu jitsu often lives in its trades. A defender may give one connection to recover another, and an attacker may release a near finish to climb into better control.

## Position Before Force

Poor submission mechanics often ask force to solve a positional problem. The attacker squeezes because the angle is wrong, cranks because the defender can rotate, pulls because the hips are not in place, and insists because the defender's posture was never broken.

Better mechanics make the finish controlled rather than explosive. The defender feels the problem earlier and more clearly, and the attacker needs less speed, less strain, and less frustration. In training, understanding isolation should make submissions safer, not more reckless.

Everyone in the room is a training partner. Everyone is there to get better and to make each other better.

Respect the tap. Apply submissions slowly and methodically. When you are off the mats, do whatever you like. In the room, respect your training partner.

For more dangerous submissions, or submissions against joints that are less resilient, control comes before everything else. The knee, heel, and wrist are obvious examples. The submission should be taught through control first: how to hold the position, how to keep the person safe, how to understand where the danger begins. Once control is established, the mechanics of the finish can be explained without turning every rep into an application. In normal training, catch and release is often the right standard.

## Training Observation

When you watch a submission, do not start at the tap. Rewind the exchange. What was isolated first: the head, the elbow, the shoulder line, the knee line, a defensive hand, a post? Once you find the first separation, the finish usually becomes less mysterious.

Isolation creates opportunity, but finishing often depends on where force bends, rotates, wedges, or strangles. The next chapter gives names to those mechanics: fulcrums and levers.
