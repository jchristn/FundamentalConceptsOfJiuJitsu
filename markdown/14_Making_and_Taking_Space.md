# Making and Taking Space

Almost every exchange comes down to space, and space changes hands in two separate steps. First you make it, then you take it. If you make space and fail to take it, you've just handed your opponent something they can claim and use against you.

On the mat I say it as a pair, "make and take," because the two are different jobs and blurring them is where good movement stalls. Making space is creating an opening: a frame that pushes the opponent off you, or a reaction you provoked that makes them move themselves. Taking space is claiming that opening before it closes, filling it with a knee, an elbow, a grip, or a hook so the opponent cannot move back in and reclaim it.

This gets its own chapter because the most common way clean movement fails is a player making space beautifully and then doing nothing with it. If you never take that space, the opponent is likely to claim it and use it against you.

## Making Space

Space gets made two ways: you create it, or the opponent hands it to you. Watch who controls the space in any exchange and you can usually tell who is going to win the position.

You create it with structure and movement. A frame between the two bodies makes room (refer back to Inside Position), a bridge lifts the hips off the mat, and a push extends the opponent away. Breaking their posture folds them forward and opens the space behind the fold. None of this has to be subtle, as long as the opening ends up big enough to claim.

The opponent hands it to you when they move. A passer who scoots the base away to kill a sweep has just given the bottom player room, and an opponent who drives forward to defend a threat leaves the space behind them open (more on this in the Newton's Third Law of Motion chapter). Either way, the space is only offered until you take it.

## Taking Space

Making space starts a clock, because the instant the opening appears you have to assume the opponent wants it back. Taking space means filling the opening with something the opponent now has to solve before they can close it again: a knee inserted inside the hips that blocks them from squaring back up, or an underhook that turns a defensive arm into control. When space becomes available, decide whether you want it, and if so, take it. To test it, ask whether the opponent can return to where they were without solving a new problem. If they can, you only borrowed the space.

## Making Without Taking It

Making space without taking it is the failure to hunt for in your own game. You set up frames under side control and use them to push the top player away, exposing a gap between your bodies. If you do nothing with that new space, you pushed for nothing.

Do not make space you are not ready to claim or capitalize on in the same breath. A frame with no follow-up knee is an alarm clock with no one there to answer it. The making and the taking should run as one motion. Alternatively, intentionally make space to make even more space, to get away.

## Both Sides of the Mat

Both players make space and take space, and both can also give space away. The first escape most people learn from bottom side control has every part of this in it. Establish frames, push away to make space, pivot the body away, and insert the bottom knee before the top player takes the space back. The frame makes the space, the pivot changes the angle and amplifies the space, and the knee claims the opening. Do the first two and forget the third and you have shown the top player exactly where you are going.

Half guard shows the same from another position. From bottom with a knee shield and the future underhook arm hidden behind it, the leg and arm shoot through together to make space, and coming up on the hand to drive that arm into an underhook takes it (the Half Guard chapter goes through this in detail). That one side of the body changes jobs in the same instant, from a frame that was only keeping distance to a lever that is now claiming ground.

The top player plays the same game in reverse. Passing is making space to advance, then taking it with pressure before the bottom player can rebuild. And a top player can hand space away too: scoot the hips and base far enough to kill a deep-half sweep and you have given the bottom player room to rebuild a stronger half guard.

## Common Misreads

Most mistakes happen around the point where making turns into taking. The most common is making space and freezing: the gap appears and the player admires it or ignores it instead of filling it. The opposite error is taking before making, trying to jam a knee or an underhook into a space that does not exist yet, and all that does is let the opponent staple the limb. A more subtle failure is claiming with the wrong structure, like a forearm-to-hip frame in bottom side control that folds the instant weight arrives, so the space was never really yours to begin with. From the top, players give space away without accounting for it: they move a base or the hips to stop one threat and ignore the room that same move opens for the opponent.

## Training Observation

Take one recovery or one pass and split it into its two beats out loud. Name the moment space was made, then name the moment it was taken, and notice the gap between them. The wider that gap, the more time the opponent had to reclaim the opening. In a good exchange the two beats sit almost on top of each other; in a failed one, you will usually find that the player made space and then abandoned it.
