# Yin and Yang

Every jiu jitsu position contains a conversation. One person is trying to create a condition. The other person is trying to prevent it, undo it, or turn it into a different problem.

Side control shows it most plainly. The top player wants to keep the bottom player flat, control the head and hips, deny inside frames, separate elbows from ribs, and make the bottom player carry weight while facing the wrong direction. The bottom player wants the opposite: turn to a side, recover inside frames, reconnect knees and elbows, clear head control, and create enough space to recover guard, come up, or escape.

That paired logic is what I mean by yin and yang. The position sets both people's jobs, so if you can name what the attacker wants, you can usually name the defender's job, and what the defender needs usually tells you what the attacker must deny.

Side control also makes the tradeoff visible, because the top player always takes one advantage and lives with a different risk. Pin 1 (head-side arm on near side), Pin 2 (head-side arm on far side), and Pin 3 (Pin 2 facing the hips), which the Side Control chapter goes through in turn, all give the top player something. So do chest-to-chest pressure, hip-to-chest pressure, perpendicular control, and angled control. Each of them also gives the bottom player a different problem to solve and a different place to look for recovery.

The bottom player's movement is the other half of the same conversation. When the top player misaligns the spine at the neck and then turns the knees, the bottom player tries to realign the head, spine, and knees. Frames and knees go the same way: the top player blocks them and the bottom player tries to insert them. Any space the bottom player makes but fails to take, the top player takes back.

## The Opposite Job

Open guard is a battle over connection, distance, angle, and hip mobility. The guard player wants grips, hooks, frames, off-balancing, and angles. The passer wants to remove connection, pin or redirect the legs, control the hips, and enter without giving away posture or base.

In closed guard the bottom player wants to break posture, create angle, pull elbows across centerline, and isolate arms or neck. Against that breakdown, the top player wants posture, safe elbows, base, and a controlled guard opening.

Under mount, the bottom player is trying to reconnect: compact arms, a protected neck, connected elbows, angle, and a usable post-trap or guard recovery. The top player is separating, working to get elbows away from ribs, knees away from elbows, and the neck or back exposed.

Back control is asymmetry against alignment. The attacker wants the defender unable to face them. The defender wants hand position, shoulder position, hip movement, and neck safety long enough to restore alignment.

In leg entanglements, one player is controlling the knee line and the other is trying to free it. Before turning or standing, the defender wants posture, safe alignment, hook removal, and knee-line freedom, while the entangling player wants hip access, knee-line control, rotational control, and off-balancing.

## Diagnosing From the Other Side

Students often ask, "What do I do from here?" A more useful question is, "What does my opponent need from here?"

If you are under mount and the top player needs your elbows separated, your first job is to protect and reconnect your elbows. Passing open guard is the same read: the guard player needs hip mobility, so your job is to pin, staple, redirect, or otherwise limit the hips before chasing upper-body control. When you are attacking the back and the defender needs hand position and shoulder alignment, the choke should not be your first thought. Control the hands and keep the shoulder line compromised.

Looking from your opponent's perspective keeps your technique selection from being random: you stop choosing moves because you like them and start choosing actions because they deny what the opponent wants.

## Mindset Before Catalog

The positional chapters in the second half of this book are not catalogs. Open guard alone could fill volumes. So could half guard, mount, back control, and leg entanglements. This book, meant to be short and conceptual, isn't going to try to drain every detail of every position. Rather, each positional chapter asks what the position is trying to become.

From top side control, are you trying to hold, advance, isolate, mount, attack, or expose the back? From bottom side control, are you trying to frame, turn, recover guard, come up, or reverse? From closed guard, is the bottom player controlling posture or just holding? Is the top player opening with structure or reaching into danger?

## Exceptions and Transitions

Good players sometimes give the opponent a temporary version of what they want in order to win something larger. To escape mount, a defender may turn and expose the back briefly. A passer may release pressure to step around a frame, and a guard player may extend a limb to create a stronger angle. Attackers do it too, abandoning a submission to climb to a better pin.

Those trades depend on owning the exposure. If you chose it, and you have timing, structure, and an exit, it can be good jiu jitsu; if it was forced on you and you have no plan, you are usually just losing the position.

Without that distinction, principles can become slogans. "Never cross your centerline" is useful for a beginner who keeps giving away arm drags or getting their back taken. It is incomplete for an advanced player who crosses deliberately to invert, attack, or transition. Principles should be used as a set of baseline rules that you, with a solid understanding of the implications, can situationally bend as needed.

## Training Observation

During one round, narrate silently. When defending, say: "They want X, so I need Y." When attacking, reverse it: "They need Y, so I must deny it, delay it, or punish it." Keep the language short enough to think while tired.

The next chapter adds pressure to this paired mindset. Once you know the opposite job, you can begin asking how to force the opponent to choose between bad options.
