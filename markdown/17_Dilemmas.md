# Dilemmas

A dilemma forces the opponent to answer one problem in a way that exposes another. Everyone has options, and having two attacks does not make a dilemma. If they defend the first threat, the second threat becomes stronger; if they defend the second, the first one returns. The opponent can know everything about the position and still be stuck, because the position makes their two best responses conflict. The best practitioners go past dilemmas to trilemmas and beyond.

## Not Just Options

Students often think of combinations as a list: armbar to triangle, triangle to omoplata, sweep to submission, pass to mount, mount to back. Lists are useful for memory, but a list becomes a dilemma only when its threats share a positional and mechanical relationship, so that the way the opponent responds to one threat opens an opportunity for another.

An armbar and a triangle become a dilemma when the defender's armbar escape gives the triangle angle and exposes the neck, and the triangle defense gives the armbar back. A sweep pairs with a submission in the same way: the post that stops the sweep exposes the arm, and hiding the arm makes the sweep easier. With a pass and a back take, the defender turns to stop hip control and gives the attacker access behind the shoulders.

## Closed Guard and the Posture Problem

In closed guard, the top player wants posture, while the bottom player wants breakdown, angle, and isolation. At first glance, the top player's job sounds straightforward: keep posture and open the guard.

If the top player's posture is broken, they become susceptible to being swept. Their head is low, their hips are hinged, their hands may be late to post, and their base can be pulled across the bottom player's hips. The bottom player can move from posture control into sweeps, triangles, armbars, collar attacks, or back exposure depending on the grips and angle.

If the top player regains posture, a different set of problems can appear. A tall posture inside closed guard can allow the bottom player to posture up to the top player, exposing kimuras, guillotines, and hip bump sweeps, most of all when the hands drift to the mat or the elbows leave safe connection from the midsection. So the top player has solved the first problem and walked into another. If they stay low, they risk being moved, bent forward, and swept; if they posture up, they risk giving the bottom player access to the arms, neck, or sit-up attacks. The guard player switches between these attacks on purpose, building a position where every correction the top player has to make carries a risk.

A two-on-one can create the same kind of conflict from a single controlled arm (refer back to Two-on-One). If the top player accepts the angle, the back starts to become visible and available. If they drive back in while the arm is still controlled, the arm under two-on-one control cannot act as a post, so the sweep becomes simple. Since each correction exposes the other branch, the grip fight turns into a positional dilemma.

The defending player can still reduce the dilemma. They need posture without exposed elbows, base without predictable posts, and guard-opening mechanics that do not give the attacker a usable angle, posture control, or an attack surface. Good defense often means refusing to address one threat so completely that the other threat becomes available to the attacker.

## How Dilemmas Are Built

Dilemmas start with control, because without it the opponent can leave or overpower the attacker. The first threat then has to be viable enough that the opponent must address it.

From closed guard, breaking posture is effective when grips, hips, and angle make the top player carry weight. Arm isolation from mount is real only when the top player can keep base while climbing. An open-guard sweep needs a post that is denied or overloaded, and the choke from the back needs hand position and shoulder control that make the defender late.

After the first threat is real, the attacker watches the response, because the response reveals the second attack. When a defender posts a hand, the arm becomes available. Turning to protect the neck improves the back angle. A passer who squares the hips lets the guard player enter under the base, and a leg-lock defender who stands to free the knee line makes the sweep available.

The attacker should not force the second attack too early. A dilemma has timing. If the attacker moves before the defender commits, then the attacker is being presumptuous and didn't allow the dilemma to mature. If the attacker moves after the defender is too deep and established in their defense of the first problem presented, the defender may recover structure. The useful moment is when the attacker catches the defender in transition, after they have committed to solving the first problem and before they have rebuilt the structure needed to solve the second.

## Positional Dilemmas

Mount is full of dilemmas. The bottom player wants their elbows connected and their neck protected to minimize the threat of a submission. The top player wants to climb, advancing position toward the head, until the elbows separate and the neck is exposed. A push to recover space makes the arms targets for an attack. Turning to hide the arms may show the top player a pathway to the back, and if the bottom player stays flat and compact, the top player may keep climbing and increase pressure to expose the elbows and neck.

High mount gives the top player strong control that holds up against defense. The top player can threaten armbars and triangles, or advance into S-mount and armbar. The bottom player has to protect the neck and hide the elbows at the same time, and those two jobs compete. A hand protecting the neck and collar may expose the elbow, while an elbow pulled tight to the body may expose a triangle opportunity for the top player. A defender who is worried about both may give the top player time to climb.

Side control creates a different version. The bottom player wants to turn in toward the opponent, establish frames, and reconnect their knee to elbow to create an inside frame to make space. The top player can pressure the head to stop the turn, separate the near elbow to attack mount or submissions, or float as the bottom player bridges and changes their angle. If the bottom player pushes, the arm may become an isolated target. If they turn hard, the knees will move, exposing the belly, and a path to the back or mount may open. If they stay flat, pressure and isolation get worse.

Back control may be the clearest hand-fighting dilemma, because the attacker wins by making one defensive priority steal time from another. The defender cannot see what the opponent is doing. They need hands to protect the neck and hands to clear hooks at the same time, and they cannot do both well. If the defender uses both hands on the choking arm, the attacker may improve hooks, trap an arm, or maintain shoulder control. If the defender focuses on the hooks, the neck defense becomes late.

Leg entanglements create dilemmas around knee line, posture, and balance. The defender wants to free the knee line, stand safely, and avoid rotational danger, while the entangling player wants off-balancing tied to line control. If the defender stands tall, the sweep may appear; if they drop weight to stop the sweep, the leg may become easier to control. You can read the position by asking which bad option the defender is being forced to choose.

## The Defender's Job

No single response solves a dilemma. The defender has to reduce the number of live threats and lower the odds of any remaining threat becoming effective. That means rebuilding posture without giving up centerline, or posting without exposing a limb.

Good defenders also recognize false dilemmas, often set as traps. Sometimes the first threat isn't real, which might be a sweep that is threatened but can't happen because the post is still available. In these cases, the defender does not need to overreact. If the submission has no isolation, the defender may not need to give up position to escape it. If the attacker has an option but doesn't have the control needed to use it, it might not be a real threat. Patience may dissolve the attack. A real dilemma becomes dangerous when the attacker has established position so deeply that any movement by the defender exposes an isolation that leads to a submission.

## Training Observation

During one round, look for moments where a defense creates the next attack. Do not ask only, "What technique did they use?" Ask, "What response did they force, and what did that response expose?"

From closed guard, watch posture. When the top player stays low, what sweep or posture-breaking threat appears? When they posture, what kimura, guillotine, hip bump, or angle appears? Then reverse the question for the top player: how can they recover posture without giving the bottom player the next attack for free?

Part Two, after one more concept, applies this idea inside major positions. Open guard, closed guard, half guard, pins, back control, and leg entanglements all become more useful when the player understands both what they want and which bad choice they are trying to force the opponent into.
