# Newton's Third Law of Motion

For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. That law is useful on the mat, because people generally answer force with force and they answer movement with movement. Once you expect that, you can feed an opponent the opposite of what you want, wait for the reaction they cannot help giving, and use it.

Newer students often feel that an upper belt is steps ahead of them, reading the match like a book they have already finished. Some of that is true, but a large part of what looks like foresight comes from knowing that to make an opponent do a certain thing, you sometimes have to ask for the opposite. The upper belt is causing the reaction more than predicting it.

## Doing the Opposite

The simplest version is push and pull: if I want someone to come toward me, I might push them away first. Most people will meet the force of me pushing them by pushing back into me to defend their base. When they push back, I stop fighting it and go with the direction they are already giving me, pulling as they come. They are supplying the movement, so they come in closer than they meant to.

Direction follows the same law. Say I am in closed guard and I want to drag my opponent to my left. Pulling them straight to my left prompts them to resist exactly where I am pulling, and a braced opponent is heavy. So I push them to my right instead. They push back against it, the way people almost always do, and that push carries them to my left under their own power. I enticed them into doing the work, so I never had to win a strength contest.

Posture is vulnerable to the same trick. From closed guard I usually want to break the opponent's posture and pull them down onto me, but pulling a postured, braced opponent straight down is a fight against their strongest structure. So I do the opposite first. I push them away and often fake a sweep, giving them something to worry about behind them. A postured opponent almost always drives back into me to defend their base and shut the sweep down. The moment they commit that drive forward, I stop pushing and pull them in hard. Their own forward pressure now breaks the posture I could not break on my own.

Most of the time I reach for this to regain or cover position, to get back to somewhere I want to be. It can also set up a submission, and the mechanism is the same either way.

## On the Feet and On Top

I have explained this from closed guard, but the law runs through the whole match, standing and on top, in attack and in defense.

Standing up does not change it (the Takedowns and the Standing Battle chapter covers the standing game). If I want to shoot a takedown, driving straight into a braced, postured opponent is the hard way in. So I snap their head down first. They do not want their head down, so they fight to posture back up, and the instant they drive up to recover their posture I change levels and shoot into the space they just cleared by defending the threat I showed them up high. The takedown lands because the snap was real enough that posturing back up was not optional. Threaten the head and the legs come open. Threaten the legs and the head comes up.

On top the law often runs between two threats instead of two directions. From mount or from the back I attack the neck. They hand-fight to defend it, and defending the neck pulls an arm away from where it was protecting, so now that arm is available. I attack the arm, they retract it to protect it, and the neck opens back up. Each threat is real enough that they have to answer it, and every answer they give uncovers the other thing I wanted. A good top game feels like it never runs out of options because each attack borrows the defense of the last.

When I pass with the torreando, I commit hard to one side, hard enough that the bottom player turns their hips and frames to shut that side down. Once they sell out to defend it, the other side is empty, and I pass where they just left. If I only threatened one side they could sit and wait, so the first threat has to be real; their defense of it is what opens the second. I do not have to be quicker than them, because I let their defense choose the side for me.

## When It Does Not Fire

Newton's Third Law, applied to jiu jitsu, depends on the opponent supplying the reaction to your action, and the reaction has to be close to involuntary in order to be reliable. Newer players supply that reaction most reliably, because they feel obligated to answer everything and have not yet learned that they do not have to. Push them and they push back. Fake a sweep and they scramble to defend it.

The bait backfires against someone who has trained long enough to instinctively know that they don't have to respond to every situation you present. If I push a fellow black belt one way hoping they pressure back in, they are just as likely to let me push them, settle wherever they end up, and calmly work from there. They never give me the reaction I was fishing for. Against that opponent the push just moves them to a neutral spot and hands them the choice of what to do next.

Without that read, this is a gimmick, because the action only earns the reaction when the opponent feels they have to answer it. The more experienced they are, the more real the threat has to be before they will respond, which is why the strongest version of this idea is a genuine dilemma, a threat they cannot ignore. A fake still works on someone who answers fakes, but a real threat works on everyone.

## Where This Shows Up

This idea runs through other chapters, and in each of them the opponent's reaction is a tool for you to use. The Konami Code sets up a direction you do not want so the opponent gives the one you do. Closed guard baits the top player into opening the guard, knee on belly reads and lies with the hands, and dilemmas force a correction that opens the next attack.

## Training Observation

For one round, stop trying to move your opponent directly and watch what they hand you instead. Push, and notice whether they push back. Threaten one thing, and notice what opens when they defend it. Do not chase the opening yet. Just name the reaction in your mind each time it comes, so you learn which pushes and threats are close to involuntary and which ones a given partner can ignore.

In the next round, start using what you saw. Pick the direction you want, ask for the opposite, and let their reaction do the work. Pay attention to who reacts and who does not. Newer partners react more automatically, while a more experienced partner needs a more real threat before giving you anything.
