# Open Guard

Open guard is any guard where the legs are not locked around the opponent's body, which makes it a very large category. It contains collar-sleeve, lasso, spider, butterfly, De la Riva, reverse De la Riva, seated guard, supine guard, shin-to-shin, single-leg entries, and many other named systems, and a person could spend years studying only one branch.

A short conceptual book has to look underneath the names. Open guard is a battle over distance, angle, connection, and hip mobility. The guard player wants meaningful connection without becoming pinned. The passer wants to remove, flatten, redirect, or step around the guard player's layers without giving away posture, base, or limbs.

The position feels endless because the tools change quickly. A foot can be a frame, a hook, a wedge, or a lever, and a knee can block distance one second and become a handle the next. A grip can break posture, deny a post, or expose an elbow, but it can also just waste energy, so the habit to build in open guard is asking what each connection is doing.

## The Guard Player's Job

Distance by itself is not control. If the passer can disengage, circle, reset grips, or choose the entry without consequence, the guard player may be safe for the moment but not yet dangerous.

Good open guard creates controlled instability. The passer is connected enough to be moved, tilted, slowed, or threatened, while the guard player's hips remain mobile enough to angle, recover, invert, sit up, or enter underneath. Knees and feet build layers between the passer and the hips, and grips and hooks should make the passer solve a problem before they can pass.

The bottom player's job is to put things in the way. As much of your body as possible should become an obstacle the passer has to solve. Feet, knees, shins, frames, grips, hooks, and hip angle are all layers that create push and pull levers, and the passer has to clear something before the pass can keep moving.

Angle comes before attack. From square alignment, a sweep often becomes a strength contest. Attack a submission before posture is broken and you may only overextend, and a back take without control of the hips or shoulders may disappear as soon as the passer turns. Keep asking whether you have created an angle or are attacking straight into their structure.

Open guard players expose limbs constantly, so centerline is in play on every exchange. A sleeve, ankle, collar, or lapel grip can be useful, but an arm that reaches too far across the body can become an arm-drag entry, and a leg extended without hip mobility can be stapled. A knee that drifts outside without a second layer can let the passer enter the space between knee and elbow.

In this book I keep open guard tied to offensive jiu jitsu. Seated and supine guards both matter, but the goal is not to collect every named guard. Establish connection for a purpose: to wrestle up, enter underneath, off-balance, or force the opponent into a version of half guard where the bottom player has real advantages.

Seated guard is the version to learn first. It asks for the fewest details before it starts making sense, puts the core concepts in play right away, and works for a wide range of ages and body types. The seated player can build height, use a backstop arm, create connection, enter a single leg, or establish shin-to-shin without needing a complicated inversion or long-distance grip system.

Shin-to-shin is a good example. Connecting your shin to the opponent's shin lets you control their leg, and from that control you can wrestle up, offset their balance, set up sweeps, or force them into a half-guard exchange. The open guard is doing its job when it either leads somewhere or creates space for you to escape.

## The Passer's Job

The passer's first job is to understand the guard player's connections: which grip breaks posture, which hook controls the hip, which foot prevents forward pressure, which knee blocks the passing lane, and which hand will stop the post if a sweep begins.

The passer's matching job is getting things out of the way. Swatting at limbs randomly will not do it; identify the obstacle that decides the next exchange, then clear it, staple it, redirect it, pin it, or make it useless. Passing begins to look simpler when the passer stops thinking about the guard name and starts asking what is in the way.

Passing open guard usually begins by degrading those connections: breaking grips, controlling the hips, and advancing past the bottom player's feet, knees, and hips. Staples are often helpful to control the bottom player's leg, by using your own shin to pin their leg to the mat. Move around the bottom player's frames after considering what their recourse will be and how you will negate it. If you remove one layer carelessly and leave the next layer stronger, the guard player will use your movement to attack or recover.

Posture and base need constant attention. Stand too tall and you become easy to off-balance. Drop the head without controlling frames and you can be snapped down, elevated, or pulled into submissions. Reaching with the arms can donate handles, and if you commit all your weight in one direction, you can be swept toward the empty space.

Pressure passing and loose passing look different, but both need the same judgment. Pressure passing asks the passer to win head and hip relationships without getting tangled, while loose passing asks the passer to move around legs without letting the guard player reconnect. In both cases, the target is the hip line, the frames, and the guard player's ability to face the passer, rather than the feet.

## The Same Job From Different Places

You can learn a hundred different guard passes. Torreando, knee slice, leg drag, double under. A student can treat them as four separate techniques to memorize, each with its own list of steps, but in reality, what's important is where the pass starts, how the pass is executed, and where the pass ends. They exist to move the opponent's lower body out of the way so the passer can advance toward strike-zone control, even when the route has to make a pit stop in a position like half guard along the way.

What separates the passes is where each one starts. The torreando is a standing pass, a hand on each leg, one-on-one against each leg. The passer moves the guard player's feet and legs to one side and advances the opposite direction. Torreando passes tend to be explosive: the legs go one way and the passer arrives on the other side before the guard player can turn back to face them or establish cross-foot control on the hips to begin inversion.

The knee slice usually starts from a seated or single-knee-up exchange, and relies on downward force with the passer's inside knee cutting across the thigh of the opponent's bottom leg.

The leg drag is a two-on-one against a single leg rather than one hand per leg, and because the passer is using two limbs to drag one leg across the body, it tends to be a forceful pass.

The double under is the situational one, taken from a more defensive posture, where the passer gets both arms inside, controlling their own strike zone, and underneath the opponent's legs, to then hoist the guard player's legs up and over.

The double under starts from the worst-looking spot and ends in one of the best. It looks defensive at the start because it begins by solving the guard player's best control, the strike zone, so that posture is the point of the pass and not a flaw in it. In closed guard and others, the bottom player is using their legs to own the passer's strike zone, the lane from armpits to hips. Getting both arms engaged and inside is how the passer takes that strike zone back, and from there the passer can swim the hands under and get beneath the legs. Once under them, the passer can lift the legs to the shoulders and turn them in whatever direction they like, which is why the double under usually finishes in side control. The legs are lifted, turned, and shifted out of the way rather than passed, and the passer collapses down onto the strike zone they have just reclaimed, underneath everything the legs were protecting.

The choice among them is situational rather than a matter of taste, decided by where the passer already is in relation to the opponent's body. Standing over open legs is torreando territory, and a seated or knee-up tangle is where the knee slice lives. A single leg isolated across the center invites the leg drag, while arms already trapped low and inside invite the double under. The read comes before the pass: the passer's position relative to the opponent's hips and legs determines which obstacle is in front of them, and that obstacle determines which pass will clear it. A student who memorizes four techniques still has to guess which one the moment calls for, whereas a student who understands the overarching goal reads the position and knows which pass is available.

Two of these passes fail in a way that shows the student never understood what the pass was for. The knee slice is downward pressure that pins the bottom player flat while the knee clears one leg. When a student turns it into a foot race, sprinting the knee across to the other side without the downward force, the bottom player gets the underhook and comes up. They treated the slice as getting around the leg when it should really be about getting through it in an irreversible way. The slice is pressure, not a sprint. The leg drag fails the same way. Its job is to put two limbs on one leg, kill that leg's power, and turn the hips away. A soft, one-armed drag never misaligns the hips, so the guard player just re-faces and recovers. The student dragged the leg but never took the hip. It is two on one, or nothing was really dragged at all.

The torreando has its own use of the opponent's reaction. Committing hard to one side so the bottom player defends it, then passing the side they abandoned, lets the guard player's own defense choose the open side. It is the same borrow-the-reaction idea that runs through the chapter on Newton's Third Law of Motion.

## Seated and Supine Guard

Seated guard and supine guard present different problems. A seated guard player may threaten wrestle-ups, arm drags, ankle picks, shin-to-shin entries, and snap-down style reactions. If the guard player can get to a seated, backstopped position, wrestling is usually attractive. The guard player can enter a single leg from seated position or build shin-to-shin connection, then establish wrestling grips on the entangled leg.

A supine guard player may use feet, hooks, inversion, elevation, and distance management to control a standing passer.

The passer must recognize the difference. Against seated guard, head position, hand fighting, and hip access become urgent because the bottom player can come up. Against supine guard, the passer often has to clear feet and knees before meaningful pressure is available. The categories overlap, and good guard players move between them, so keep asking what connection is controlling you and what angle it creates.

## Gi and No-Gi Notes

Gi grips can create long-lasting connections. Collars, sleeves, lapels, and pant grips may let the guard player hold posture or deny posts with less body contact. No-gi usually demands more direct hooks, clamps, wrist control, head position, and hip connection because grips are harder to maintain.

The clothing changes the tools and leaves the principles alone. A grip that controls nothing is useless in either one, and a hook without angle is temporary. A passer with good posture, base, and hip control is difficult to sweep with or without a gi.

## Common Misreads

Guard players often confuse contact with control, holding grips that cannot move the passer or placing feet that cannot manage distance, and they attack before creating angle or imbalance. Passers make the opposite mistakes. They try to pass the feet while the hips remain free, lower the head before clearing frames, or break one grip and ignore the hook that controls the exchange.

Both players should track connection, angle, and hip mobility. If the guard player has all three, offense appears; if the passer removes them, the guard begins to collapse.

## Training Observation

During open guard rounds, ignore named techniques for a few minutes. Track only three questions: who controls the connection (generally dominant grips), who owns the angle, and whose hips are free? Those answers will tell you more than the guard name.
