# Closed Guard

Closed guard occurs when the bottom player locks the legs around the top player's torso or hips. The position gives the bottom player a kind of control that is rare in grappling: the person underneath can hold the person on top in place while attacking posture, arms, neck, balance, and centerline.

That control is often misunderstood, because closed legs are not the same thing as a dominant guard. A bottom player who only squeezes may slow the top player down without creating real danger, while a top player who believes they are safe because they are above may discover quickly that bad posture inside closed guard is a liability.

Closed guard is a fight between posture and breakdown. The top player wants alignment, safe elbows, base, and a responsible guard opening mechanism that doesn't compromise them or create exposure. The bottom player wants to break posture, create angle, pull limbs across centerline, and turn control into isolation.

## The Bottom Player's Job

The bottom player should not treat closed guard as a parking spot, because the locked legs create time and connection but do not finish the work. Yes, this position can be used defensively and to rest tactically, but I encourage you to look at all positions (including bottom closed guard) as an offensive position, rather than a defensive or even neutral one.

The first major task is posture control: if the top player's spine is aligned, elbows are safe, hands are disciplined, and base is stable, attacks become harder. The bottom player must make that structure worse. Pull the head, climb the legs, and break the line between head, spine, and hips; use grips to prevent the top player from returning to a strong posture. Make the top player carry weight in a direction they did not choose, generally downward.

The angle is the next piece. Many closed guard attacks fail because the bottom player attacks from a position of being square. A triangle and an armbar both need an angle. Sweeps often need the top player's base shifted or a post denied, and yes, an angle of some sort on the part of the bottom player. Even when the attack begins straight on, the finish usually improves when the bottom player changes the relationship of the hips to the top player's shoulders, that is, establishes an angle.

Centerline gives the bottom player a clear target. Pulling an elbow across the body can expose the back, open triangles, create arm drags, threaten kimuras, and make posture recovery harder. The elbow does not need to travel far before the shoulder and spine begin to follow.

The bottom player should be looking for dilemmas, not isolated tricks. When the top player's posture is broken, they are easier to sweep, load, and move over a fulcrum. When the top player postures back up, the bottom player should be ready to follow them into the space they create. Kimuras, guillotines, hip bump sweeps, triangles, chokes, and posture-based sweeps all come out of that exchange.

There does not have to be a single first-choice dilemma. Collar-sleeve grip alone opens a world of opportunity: scissor sweep, triangle, armbar, kimura, and guillotine all become natural branches. None of them is guaranteed, but the top player now has a connected problem to answer.

A two-on-one can create a similar hub from a different kind of control. I credit this system and the concepts behind it to Rudy Sanchez at Smash Gyms. When the bottom player owns one arm with two useful controls, that arm can stop being a post and start becoming a handle. The side is whichever arm is in front of you. In a basic gi version, one hand can take a pistol grip at the end of the sleeve while the other cups the tricep or C-grips behind the elbow. With a hip turn, body angle, and extension of both arms, I can shift that arm away and start seeing the back.

The two-on-one puts the top player in a dilemma. If they accept the angle, I can come up to my knees and grab around the body, or come up to my elbow and pull the hips into back control. If they drive back into me while I still own the arm, the near-side post can disappear underneath them. Now the back take and the sweep are tied to the same grip fight.

A strong overhook is yet another. After having broken the top player's posture, the bottom player establishes a strong overhook on one of the arms and pivots the body (read: creates an angle) to the side of the entangled arm. From this entanglement, the bottom player can attack omoplatas, triangles, kimuras, wrist locks, and more.

## The Top Player's Job

The top player should respect closed guard as a dangerous position. The goal is to rebuild posture safely, protect centerline, open the guard with structure, and begin passing only after the legs are open, not to win a strength contest against the legs. In the vast majority of cases, for the bottom player to sweep you or attempt a submission, their guard must open, which presents opportunities for the top player to bait the bottom player into an attack for the express purpose of opening the guard.

A top player with posture is harder to attack, better able to begin a passing sequence, and better able to disengage when leaving is the right call. Getting away is a viable option. Students sometimes treat retreat as failure, but there are moments when creating distance, standing, or clearing the position is exactly the right decision. Perhaps the top player has a speed and reflex advantage, and the bottom player has a strength or technical advantage. In exactly that scenario, disengagement is smart because it re-anchors the position on situations that appeal to the advantages of the top player.

Dominant grips come next. If the bottom player has dominant grips, a sweep or submission is probably coming. The top player should not be casual about that grip fight: a collar grip can become posture control or choking pressure, and a sleeve grip can remove a post, open triangles, or pull an elbow across centerline. Before the top player worries about a favorite guard opening, they need to know whose grips are winning.

Not every attack requires opening the guard, but enough of them do that the possibility should shape the top player's thinking. When the bottom player opens the legs, they are creating opportunity for themselves, but they are also releasing a form of control. The top player can take advantage of that moment if they are organized.

Opening the guard usually happens in one of two ways. Either the top player baited the bottom player into opening it, or the top player used physics well enough to force the feet to uncross and the control to release. Seated posture is the best place to start, ahead of one-knee-up and standing openings, because it exposes the core opening problem with the fewest moving parts.

The details change between seated posture, one knee up, and standing openings, but in all three, do not pry randomly with the arms. Use structure, posture, grips, and angle so the guard has to open, with the hands exercising hip or upper-body control. Use the entirety of your body.

Keep the elbows safe. Hands posted on the mat may feel stable for a moment, but they can become targets. Elbows that drift across centerline can become arm drags, triangles, shoulder locks, and back exposure. The top player needs the arms to help with posture and guard-opening mechanics without donating them to the bottom player.

Spinal alignment is the other half of posture. A top player folded forward inside closed guard has difficulty seeing, breathing, hand fighting, and opening the legs. A top player standing with the hips disconnected from the spine may be swept or pulled into attacks. Sitting upright is not enough; good posture is organized structure that can survive grips, pulls, hip movement, and angle changes.

Opening the guard should come before passing. Students often try to pass closed guard by reaching, pushing, driving, or stacking while the bottom player's legs remain locked. That usually gives the bottom player extended arms, broken posture, and predictable pressure.

## Top Grips

The top player's grips exist to serve one mission: getting past the feet, knees, and hips. A grip that does not help clear those obstacles or protect posture is wasted, and inside closed guard a wasted grip is dangerous. Four grip families do most of the useful work.

Grips on the knees address the first obstacle. Controlling a knee lets the top player manage the frame the bottom player builds with the legs, steer where the knees can point, and prepare to clear the legs once the guard opens.

Grips on the hips or waist address the engine. The hips are where the bottom player's offense lives, because they climb, angle, lift, and turn. A grip that pins or slows the hips takes away the movement that feeds sweeps and angle before the bottom player can use it.

The lapel grip, both hands holding the same-side or both lapels with one hand oriented closer to the head and one closer to the stomach, is a posture and control grip that applies downward pressure through the sternum. It helps keep the top player's own structure organized, denies the bottom player the collar, and gives a line of control down the center without leaving the arm loose in space, susceptible to attacks like the armbar.

The C-grip under the armpits, thumbs up, controls the upper body and the bottom player's frames. Thumbs up is not a small detail. It keeps the elbow structured and the wrist strong, and it keeps the hand out of the weaker, rolled position that the bottom player can attack.

Two rules run through all four. Keep the hands closer to the bottom player's feet, knees, and hips than to the mat, and keep them closer to your own body than out in space where they can be isolated. A top grip should solve a problem the bottom player is creating; one that is only holding cloth is a liability.

## Hands on the Mat: The Good, the Bad, and the Ugly

The same posted hand can be smart, wasteful, or fatal depending on where it is and why it is there. I often tell my students that if they create a scenario from bottom closed guard that forces my hands to the mat, and they hear the "clap" of my hands hitting the mat, to call out "point!". Generally your hands need to be on your opponent, and if they can disengage your hands, they can often create opportunities for advancement, sweeps, or submissions.

The good of having your hands on the mat is a brief, purposeful post. Sometimes the top player needs a hand down to save base against a sweep, to steady a passing position for a beat, or to stop being tipped into a quadrant. When the post is short, outside the bottom player's reach, and picked back up the moment its job is done, it is ordinary base. These interactions between your hands and the mat should be brief and purposeful. Otherwise they create an opening for the bottom player.

The bad is the resting hand. A hand that lives on the mat out of habit, inside the bottom player's reach, has stopped being base and started being bait. It lowers posture, it invites the bottom player to trap or drag it, and it usually means the top player has lost the grip fight without noticing. It's even more dangerous if the bottom player has an angle and entangles your hand. In such cases you can expect a triangle, armbar, or omoplata to follow.

The ugly is a hand on the mat near or across the bottom player's centerline. That is the arm the bottom player wants most. It feeds arm drags, triangles, armbars, and back exposure, and paired with broken posture it is close to handing the bottom player an attack. A hand goes to the mat to buy a moment of base, not to live there, and never where the bottom player can reach it across the center.

## Head Height, Hip Height, Grips, and Posture and Base

Head height tells both players who is winning the posture battle. Lowering the top player's head can be powerful, but only if the bottom player connects that head control to angle, grips, or isolation. Pulling the head down and then lying flat may exhaust the bottom player more than it threatens the top player.

Hip position gives the bottom player offense, and a closed guard with dead hips stalls. For the top player, the hips must support posture and guard-opening mechanics: if they rise without alignment, the top player becomes easy to tilt, and if they stay low without progress, the bottom player may keep breaking posture.

Grips decide whether posture stays broken or returns. Bottom grips should control movement, deny posts, or isolate limbs. Top grips (refer back to Top Grips and Hands on the Mat above) should support posture and opening mechanics without exposing the arms. Holding cloth or wrists is not enough; the grip has to solve a problem.

Base is more subtle inside closed guard because the top player may be kneeling or standing and the bottom player is underneath. Still, the top player needs base against sweeps and posture breaks, and the bottom player needs hip base to turn, climb, and create angles.

The scissor sweep shows why this cannot be reduced to one post: if the bottom player controls the sleeve-side arm, the top player's hand post may be gone. But the top player can still extend the knee outward and post through the lower quadrant. The bottom player then has to change the problem: break posture, load the top player onto the hip, and move them toward a quadrant the knee post no longer protects.

Making and taking space (refer back to Making and Taking Space) runs through everything in closed guard. Space can be created by your action or by the opponent's movement. Either way, you benefit only if you reclaim that space on your terms, with grips and pressure that make life difficult for the other person.

From closed guard bottom, break the top player's posture. When they apply force to regain posture, space is being made. Follow them up with your upper body, build height onto your back elbow, and trap the cross-body shoulder. Now the posture recovery has fed a kimura threat, a hip bump sweep, or another sit-up attack.

From closed guard top, opening the guard creates space. That space has to be taken with purpose. Drop your level. Keep one grip on the hip and bring the elbow in so it can frame against one leg and prevent a clean re-guard. Establish an outside scoop grip on the other leg, usually the bottom leg, and pull it into your chest so the bottom player's movement becomes constrained.

The same dilemma remains under all of that. The top player has to decide whether to posture, fight grips, extend the base backward, stay low, or open the guard. If the bottom player is connected and ready, each choice should give them something to attack.

## Rule Set and Striking Context

Sport jiu jitsu, self-defense training, and MMA all change the urgency of closed guard without changing what each player wants: bottom wants posture broken and isolation available, and top wants posture restored and the guard opened safely. Under strikes, posture and head position become even more urgent for both players. In points-based sport jiu jitsu, closed guard can be used to slow, control, attack, or force openings under different strategic pressures. Gi grips may make posture breaking and stalling dynamics different from no-gi. But if strikes are involved, it is imperative that the bottom player break posture to minimize the number of joints involved in strikes sent by the top player, thus reducing their efficacy.

## Common Misreads

The bottom player often squeezes the legs and waits for the top player to make a mistake, or attacks from flat hips. The top player often tries to open with the arms instead of posture, hips, and base, or stands without first dealing with grips and centerline.

Closed guard becomes clearer when both players ask the same question from opposite sides: can the top player stay aligned, or can the bottom player break that alignment and turn it into isolation?

## Training Observation

In closed guard positional rounds, spend the first minute ignoring submissions. Bottom player: break posture and create angle. Top player: rebuild posture and protect elbows. Once that battle is visible, the attacks and openings will make more sense.
