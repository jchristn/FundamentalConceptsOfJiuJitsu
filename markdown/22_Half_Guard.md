# Half Guard

Half guard occurs when the bottom player controls one of the top player's legs between their own legs while the top player has partially passed the guard. That definition makes the position sound smaller than it is. Half guard is a family of positions that vary widely in who is winning.

In one version, the top player has a crossface, underhook, heavy hips, and a knee nearly free, and the pass is close. In another, the bottom player is on a side, up on an elbow, has an underhook with strong head position, and access to the hips, and the top player may be one step from being swept, wrestled up on, or forced to expose the back. Deep half guard is different again: the bottom player is almost fully underneath the top player with a tight scoop grip underneath the far leg and about to apply a sweep, lockdown, or an electric chair.

Half guard sits between guard and pass, which makes the mindset easy to misread. The top player should not assume they are winning because they are above and part of the way through a pass, any more than the bottom player should assume they are safe because they still have control over one of the top player's legs.

Half guard can look like a giant flowchart, with more pathways than a chapter could document. This chapter explains why the pathways appear: who owns head height, who owns connection, what is in the way, what has been removed, where the weight can travel, and whose center of gravity can be loaded.

## The Bottom Player's Job

Bottom half guard usually begins with one command: do not accept being flattened. That generally translates to "keep your shoulders off the mat".

Flat half guard can become miserable because the top player can connect head control, shoulder pressure, underhooks, and weight from the chest and hips. Once the bottom player's spine is turned away, the trapped leg may no longer function as control. It becomes the last piece delaying the pass, and a weak one at that.

The bottom player needs to keep from being flattened, which implies a side angle, inside position, and a useful relationship to the trapped leg. The shoulders should be vertical, which means lying on your side with one shoulder oriented above the other instead of both shoulders facing the ceiling. Flat shoulders mean you are on your back, and if you are on your back in half guard, you are usually getting smashed.

Vertical shoulders make the position alive. You can crunch in and extend away quickly and resist the top player's effort to flatten you, and most escapes, sweeps, and attacks from bottom half guard depend on not being flat. The hips are the other half of that job. They are the strongest part of the body, and they can almost always raise off the ground from bottom half guard, particularly when you have vertical shoulders. If the top player uses pressure to begin to roll you from vertical shoulders toward flat shoulders, you can elevate the hips, get the bottom arm underneath or behind you, and slide the upper body back over that arm as a lever to recover the side angle. From there it is very easy to come up on your bottom elbow, which creates a pathway to come up on your bottom hand.

From that side angle, the Konami Code chapter becomes practical. The bottom player can build height, attack the outside arm, threaten the head and collar, work toward an underhook, or move toward deep half, among others. Flat shoulders make the same half guard feel like a slow pass. When you're flat, your first job is to not be flat. When you're not flat, you have more freedom to advance position.

The lesson travels immediately to pins. The Konami Code is only useful when the position is alive enough to support direction. In side control, mount, and back control, the same diagnostic applies in a harsher form, and the first job is often to make the position alive again. If your head is controlled, your shoulders are flattened, your limbs are no longer obstacles, and your body cannot turn, the directions may exist in theory but not in practice.

In half guard the answer varies: an underhook and head position for a wrestle-up, a knee shield to manage distance, getting underneath the hips, or recovering full guard by retracting the entangling leg. Each one depends on structure. The bottom player needs to make the trapped leg an anchor for offense or recovery instead of a place to hide, and being on your side gives you options from which to work.

The knee shield is a fantastic position for managing distance from an opponent. The high and low variations have different advantages, and both can be used to drive your opponent away. Hooking with the outside foot is a great way to also pull your opponent in, particularly when coupled with an effective grip.

The knee shield is also a great tool for hiding the arm that will eventually go for the underhook. That arm can stay hidden underneath the knee of the knee shield, inside the thigh, until the bottom player is ready to change the relationship. When I want distance, I use the knee shield. When I want to be close and have connection, the knee shield either drives through or comes out entirely so I can focus on another form of connection: the underhook, control of the leg, or a wrestle-up path.

That transition is often a speed issue. I should abandon the knee shield while I am advancing to the next connection. The top player may win that race. If they beat me to head control or the crossface before my underhook, leg control, or wrestle-up connection is real, I may have traded a useful distance tool for a flattened half guard.

The textbook half guard example of making and taking space starts from that same knee shield. I can be on bottom with collar-sleeve grips, with the collar-grip arm hidden underneath the knee shield. I shoot the knee through and the arm goes with it at the same time. As that happens, I release the sleeve grip and come up on that hand. My body (hips, chest) gets closer to them as the knee shield leg shoots through and the arm follows into the underhook. The making of space is the leg and arm shooting through. The taking of space is following that movement, getting the underhook, and pinning my head and body to their chest, abdomen, or hip.

It is also a good frame-to-lever example. The collar-grip arm starts as part of a shielded defensive structure. It helps keep distance and keeps the top player from freely owning the space. When the knee shield comes out and the arm becomes the underhook, that same side of the body has changed jobs. It has stopped only putting something in the way and started taking the space back.

If I lose the race and the top player wins the crossface, I still have choices. I can back away and scoot back, using the top arm as a wedge. Or I can accept that they are pressuring in and use that pressure to scoot underneath them, either to deep half or to load their center of gravity on top of mine and sweep or roll them. Losing the race is not the same as losing the position, but it does mean I need to stop pretending I still have the same knee-shield problem.

An underhook is not magic. The body goes where the head leads, so an underhook without head position may get smashed: if the top player drives the head down, the spine and shoulders follow it to the mat and the underhook goes with them. With a flat spine it may fail to lift, and if it does not connect to hip movement it may only be a grip. The underhook becomes powerful when it helps the bottom player escape the head or establish height, turn the top player's body, access the hips, or threaten the back.

My most reliable bottom half guard sequence, one of the first things Ron taught me, begins with a knee shield, hiding the top arm while it holds a collar grip, advancing to the underhook. That underhook is almost always on the side opposite the entangled leg, i.e. I hold the underhook with my left arm while my right leg is entangling their leg in half guard. I take the space that was previously occupied by the knee shield and underhook with my head and body, connecting tightly to my opponent. Using the underhook arm, I shuck them forward and in the direction over my head to clear my head under the underhook side, and establish head height and hip height to "come out the back door" and attack the back. To make the position even more powerful, my outside non-entangling leg can be brought inside to work in conjunction with my entangling leg to pull their entangled leg foot toward my body, which begins to change the alignment of their spine and the direction their body is pointing.

When you establish an underhook, it should generally be low and deep across their belt line. I typically do not want a loose mid-back or upper-back underhook. I'm confident situations exist where they are optimal, but for the majority of applications, a low and deep underhook is preferred. I want the arm around the waist line, ideally at or below the belt line, with my hand reaching to the opposite side of the body. From that half guard underhook hub, the bottom player has a lot of advantages against the top player. They are not flat, they have mobility, and they are controlling the top player's strike zone. They can execute forceful movements against the top player, with live paths to escape to the back, to wrestle up, and to establish height.

Looking at the Konami Code from the underhook position, forward is often the simplest option: drive through the opponent. The underhook is already on the waist, so there is leverage. The near leg is entangled. The far leg may be reachable with the bottom arm or by your own outside foot, though that can sacrifice head height. That top leg can create a fulcrum behind the opponent and make the drive more powerful.

The top non-entangled leg can attack the opponent's trapped foot from the inside and drag it toward your own groin. That starts to extend and turn the knee away from you. This often requires a fulcrum, one that I like to create using my entangling leg, at my opponent's entangled knee. Once the knee turns, the spine turns. Once the spine turns, the head turns away, and the back begins to appear. The common reactions are an overhook with top pressure to the head, or the top player trying to come up on the trapped foot so the knee turn no longer misaligns them the same way. That reaction usually happens while I still have an underhook. Even if the underhook is hard to hold, I can drop it down to a single leg and entangle that leg with my own legs. I prefer seated single-leg control from there, using my outside leg to make the takedown more effective. If I can grab the far-side foot, the takedown is imminent.

Another branch is the shuck. I like to call it the "chicken wing": the underhook arm flings the top player forward toward the quadrant that is over my head. For that to work, the grip should stay low around the waist, reaching toward the far side of the body. My head is buried into their stomach near the hip, and my hips try to connect to the outside of their near-side hip. I literally want them to faceplant and eat the mat. If they faceplant, my head height appears immediately and their back is exposed. The entangling leg is already an instant back-control hook. From there, the top leg can come over to the other side, the seat belt can be established across the body, and the far-side hook can come in for back control (the Back Control chapter goes further into this).

My favorite half guard dilemma is the far-side arm two-on-one from bottom with vertical shoulders. I prefer C grips here: thumbs behind the opponent's arm, fingers above the arm. I am looking at my fingers. If I control that far-side arm and can come up on my bottom elbow, the kimura becomes easy. Usually their movement dictates whether I go to the kimura or the underhook. The dilemma is kimura or underhook: attack the arm and isolate the shoulder, or build height and steal the strike zone. I do not treat it as kimura or nothing.

Deep half is usually not my first choice from that hub. I tend to go there when the top player establishes a whizzer and their base is too far away for me to sweep directly. In that case, I can dive underneath, bring my bottom arm under their far-side thigh, and capture a scoop grip. Their whizzer has already taken away their outside post. I can load their weight onto me, flatten out enough to tilt them toward my outside, and sweep. I have to escape my legs during the sweep so I land on top in side control instead of landing in half guard.

Deep half becomes a trap when the top player drops their weight and moves their base away. If their hips are far enough away, the sweep can become difficult or impossible. The bottom player may feel stuck underneath with the top player's weight settled in a place that does not move. Should I get stuck underneath, my options begin with where my opponent's center of gravity is. If their center of gravity is directly over mine, or if I can scoot underneath until their center of gravity is over mine (usually their belly button is over mine), the log roll is available. Load the top player's weight and center of gravity onto your own, eliminate the far-side post, and roll through. If their base is too far away, the log roll may not be there yet. Even if the log roll fails, usually due to a strong post by my opponent, I can adjust angles and choose another quadrant, or, begin to come up on my bottom elbow (the non-entangling arm), or begin to escape out the back.

Yet another option, particularly useful when I'm stuck on bottom, is the lockdown. It gives me the option to pull the top player in, push them away, or otherwise maneuver their body. Rebuilding vertical shoulders and the log roll are outcomes. Lockdown extension and aligning the centers of gravity are mechanisms that help me get to those outcomes.

Do not memorize the branches. See why the underhook changed the position: it won inside position, helped recover head height, and gave the bottom player access to the top player's hips, knee, far foot, and back. It also forced the top player into a dilemma. They can pressure to flatten, pull away to deny the underhook, pummel back inside, whizzer, or accept a wrestling exchange, but each response opens a different direction.

Wrestling up is one of the main reasons bottom half guard can be so dangerous. From open guard, I do not usually want to force half guard against a standing opponent unless I have posture control. Going to half guard on a standing opponent is basically inviting them to pass as you're trying to establish the half guard because while standing, the opponent has too many advantages against your legs. But if collar-sleeve control can force one knee to the mat, establishing half guard becomes a very different, real, and valid option. If that posture control is not available, wrestling remains the primary option.

From bottom half guard, the bottom player has an equal footing, strong mobility, sweeps, submissions, the ability to wrestle to top position, and good options to get away. That breadth is why I love this position. Even though you are technically "on bottom," you hold far more options than the opponent on top. The opponent's choices are narrower: pass, get away, or submit, and there are relatively few effective submissions from top half compared with the bottom player's menu.

## The Top Player's Job

In top half guard, the passer's job is to convert partial progress into stable control. The top player has passed one leg but not the whole guard. Rushing from here can give the bottom player exactly what they want.

The top player's first priority is generally to flatten the bottom player out, which is also what makes vertical shoulders so critical for the bottom player. Flattening damages the bottom player's alignment: a crossface can turn the head away, an underhook can prevent the bottom player from coming up, and hip pressure can make the trapped leg less useful. A top player with the knee trapped is often still in danger, even if the upper body feels dominant, so freeing the knee line remains a priority.

Conceptually, the top player has to win head and shoulder position, prevent the bottom player from getting to a side or underneath, free the knee, free the foot, then settle or advance. The order may change, but all of those problems still have to be solved. If the top player attacks the head while the knee is deeply trapped, the bottom player may recover or elevate. If the top player frees the knee but loses inside frames and head position, the bottom player may rebuild guard or come up.

To pass half guard, the top player ties pressure to freeing the leg, controlling the hips, and denying the bottom player height. If the bottom player wins the underhook, the top player has to break posture and control head height immediately. Either of the top player's arms can become a lever against the back of the bottom player's head, and the arm on the side of the underhook can become a whizzer, which can shut the underhook down when applied correctly and safely. Head height is what makes the underhook alive. Drive the head to the mat and the underhook gets neutered.

Top half guard is how the Konami Code gets shut down. Control the head and break shoulder position. I usually take the outside arm over the top of their face and neck and drive pressure downward. Once the head is on the mat, I can move to a crossface or a similar control and begin pinning the shoulders. If I can force the head to the mat, I can get the shoulders to the mat. If I get the shoulders to the mat, the bottom player's directional options become mostly theoretical. Up, down, left, right, forward, backward, and diagonal all depend on posture, angle, and mobility, so their options are reduced largely to one: get the shoulders off the mat.

That preference also explains why I tend to avoid standing passes from top half guard. Standing can make sense in some contexts, but it often breaks the connection to the bottom player. Against a good wrestler, that lost connection may be all they need to stand up, wrestle up, or turn the exchange back into a fight I did not need to give them.

My preferred family from top half is pressure passing, or "smash passing". Pressure passing applies control to the bottom player while systematically advancing toward the pass. I keep my body close to theirs, continually applying downward and forward force. My grips inch forward and up the back. My knee systematically clears entanglement while pressuring down on their entangling leg, eventually becoming a staple holding their bottom leg to the mat. My outside non-entangled leg instep (the top of the foot) is used as a lever to then free my entangled foot. For the bottom player, it absolutely sucks. It is defeating. They have someone on top of them applying force, limiting their movement, and inch by inch advancing against them. It is humiliating and painful. It takes the wind out of their sails. That is the point of the pressure. The goal is not cruelty. The bottom player should feel their choices disappearing as the pass advances, and demoralizing them along the way makes future positions easier.

After I win head and shoulder control, I am usually using the instep of my outside leg to begin freeing the entangled leg inch by inch. The pace and the control count for more than the foot does here. The top player should not try to explode the leg free while the bottom player still has angles and posts available. Pressure passing advances by removing a little more, settling a little more, taking away the next answer, then moving again.

Smash pressure is more than being heavy. Being heavy and dropping downward can work, but driving in and through often creates more meaningful pressure, because the pressure lives in angles and fulcrums. If I can create the right angle and drive through the bottom player, the pressure multiplies. If I just put weight straight down in a bad direction, a good bottom player may build a frame, recover a knee shield, turn to a side, or load me.

A whizzer is another option. On top, a whizzer can minimize the underhook by applying overhooking pressure and making it harder for the bottom player to come up. But the whizzer gives something up. It can remove one of the top player's own posts. From bottom, if the top player whizzers my underhook arm, I can take my bottom hand and grab the end of the sleeve on their whizzer arm. Their arm is wrapped around my top arm, and their hand is usually near my stomach. Once I control that hand, they cannot post it out to stop a sweep. The top player has to evaluate the hips and base: if the hips are oriented too close to the bottom player, the sweep may be easy. If the hips and base are angled away, the force required to finish the sweep can become too great.

That whizzer fight is also a two-on-one problem when the bottom player starts controlling the whizzer arm. The top player is trying to kill the underhook, while the bottom player is trying to turn that whizzer into a missing post, a sweep, or a path underneath.

If the bottom player has gone deep and wants the log roll, the top player can answer by scooting the hips away and getting the base away. A base that is far away prevents a multitude of sweeps, but it gives the bottom player space they can take to recover to a stronger half guard position.

## Head Height, Hip Height, Grips, and Posture and Base

Head position often tells the truth first. If the bottom player's head is in a strong position and the spine can come up, the top player is in a fight. If the top player's head and shoulder pressure turn the bottom player away, the bottom player may be late even with a leg trapped.

Hip position determines whether the trapped leg controls anything. The bottom player's hips need angle, movement, or underneath access. The top player's hips need to stay heavy enough to prevent elevation but mobile enough to free the knee and foot, because heavy hips that never free the leg can become a stall and mobile hips that float too high can become sweepable.

Grips in half guard should be judged by what they change. Crossfaces, underhooks, wrist control, hip frames, lapel grips, and far-side controls influence alignment and direction; generic holding wastes time.

Losing base is dangerous for both players. The top player must avoid being rolled, elevated, or pulled into single-leg style exchanges. The bottom player must avoid collapsing under pressure or extending so far that the top player can pummel, flatten, and pass.

The guard-passing problem is the same here: passing guard is getting things out of the way. Bottom guard is putting things in the way. The passer wants feet, knees, and hips cleared, stapled, redirected, or made useless, and the same for frames, hooks, and grips. The bottom player wants as much body as possible in the way. Create obstacles. Create push and pull levers. Frames and the knee shield are excellent examples because they make the passer solve a problem before the pass can continue.

## Common Misreads

The bottom player's misreads start with clamping the leg and accepting being flat. Winning an underhook does not help if the head stays low or the shoulders get driven flat, because the top player can smash the spine back to the mat. The knee shield can stay after it is time to connect, or come out before another connection is ready, and a bottom player who loses the race between knee shield and connection may keep fighting the old problem instead of backing away with a wedge or scooting underneath the pressure. On top, the misreads include attacking the head and forgetting the knee is still trapped, standing to pass and losing connection so a good wrestler has a chance to stand, and moving the hips away to prevent deep-half sweeps without accounting for the space that gives back. The top player can also shift too high over the bottom player's head and send the weight forward too early, or allow the bottom player to get back on a side or recover head height. When that happens, the pressure is gone and the bottom player has started winning battles again.

Half guard becomes easier to read when you name the battles instead of the variation: flat or on a side, underhook or crossface, knee trapped or knee free, hips mobile or pinned, head winning or head lost.

## Training Observation

During half guard rounds, pause after each exchange and describe the position without using the words "half guard." Say what is actually happening: "bottom on side with underhook," "top head control with shoulders flattening," "bottom knee shield with distance," "bottom connected with underhook," "top knee free, foot trapped."

If that feels too abstract, make it a reset rule. Start with bottom vertical shoulders. Top wins the reset by putting the head and shoulders on the mat. Bottom wins the reset by keeping vertical shoulders long enough to choose knee shield for distance, underhook for connection, or far-side arm two-on-one for the kimura/underhook dilemma. Add a separate race round from knee shield: bottom tries to remove the shield and arrive at underhook, leg control, or wrestle-up connection; top tries to win head control or crossface first. Add a deep-half round where both players name whose center of gravity is over whose. Bottom wins by aligning the centers enough to log roll or by using lockdown extension to rebuild vertical shoulders. Top wins by moving the hips and base away without giving the bottom player space they can take.
