# Side Control

Side control occurs when the top player has passed the guard and controls the opponent from the side, usually chest-to-chest or chest-to-upper-body, without being inside the legs. It is one of the first dominant positions students learn, and one of the first positions where bad language can slow their development.

Side control is not a static hold. It is a battle over whether the bottom player can turn and rebuild structure or the top player can keep them flat, misaligned, and isolated. The best pins from top involve control of both sides of the body, that is, applying control over the head and neck, and over the hips. You can use your hands, forearms, head, shins, thighs, practically any part of your body to establish control over the body.

Escapes become late and exhausting when the bottom player is flat, with the head controlled, elbows separated, and hips pinned. Once that player can turn, recover inside frames, reconnect knee and elbow, and redirect pressure, the top player's control starts to loosen.

## The Top Player's Job

Top side control begins with control of the head and hips. Head control sets spinal direction; hip control governs guard recovery and movement. A top player who controls only the chest may feel heavy, but the bottom player may still turn, frame, pummel, or recover the legs.

I personally love side control, and it has a lot to do with how Rudy explained his perspective on it to me. Mount has a wealth of options (the Mount chapter covers them), but I often find side control easier to control from and easier to find submissions from. When I am fully on top of the bottom player's hips or chest in mount, their recovery problem can become smaller and more focused. Side control is more dynamic. The bottom player has more possible reactions, but that also means the top player has more ways to redirect those reactions into pressure, pins, submissions, and transitions.

Flattening the bottom player limits movement and makes it possible to misalign the spine. The bottom player wants to get to a side, so the top player often wants to pin both shoulders flat to the mat while making the head face away from the escape. Crossface pressure, underhooks, hip blocks, near-side controls, and far-side isolation are all ways of doing that.

A useful pressure example is shoulder pressure across the jaw and down. Straight downward pressure may be heavy, but it can leave the bottom player organized enough to bridge, turn, or frame. Positionally, by applying that downward pressure, it also limits the mobility of the top player. Pressure across the jaw changes where the head points and misaligns the spine, so the bottom player is carrying weight while facing the wrong direction. It makes the position suck.

My preferred side-control style starts with strike-zone control. Side control only functions if the top player controls the space from armpit to hip. That control gives the top player influence over the hips, limits movement, and creates advancement and submission opportunities. Without it, top pressure may feel heavy for a moment but fail to decide where the bottom player can go.

The way Rudy taught me to organize and categorize side control positions is through the following three pin positions.

Pin 1 has the head-side arm on the near side of the head. It is effective for control, more so when there is strong contact, chest-to-chest pressure, and the bottom player's spine is misaligned. It is also the first pin an early student should understand because it is the most instinctual version of side control: grab the head, grab the body, and put pressure down. The strength and limitation lies in the fact that one hand is on each side of the body, making Pin 1 very good for control and pressure, but making it weak in submission potential.

I do not like to stay in Pin 1 forever. Hugging and stalling can be useful for a moment because they reset the tempo of the match and give everyone a chance to think. A predictable tempo is also easier to disrupt. But Pin 1 should be a placeholder on the way to Pin 2, knee on belly, direct mount, side-back control, or a rodeo into S-mount. Staying in Pin 1 only makes sense if you are restraining someone or, in competition, running out the final seconds while already ahead. Even then, I would discourage making that the habit.

Pin 1 is a natural landing spot when passing the guard, whether it is a pressure pass (hug around the chest), an arm between the legs pass, or even a knee slide. It's a great pin and position for "cooking" (make it suck!) people when there is an intention to move toward submission. Cooking means controlling and applying pressure long enough to mentally and physically drain their gas tank. Watch the tempo: when the bottom player stops being explosive and settles into the position, you can feel that the pace changed. Once I know I have control, and I feel my opponent has become disheartened or their will has broken positionally, I advance.

Pin 2 has the head-side arm on the far side of the head but above the far shoulder. It is still strong for control, but it opens a much richer attacking surface. In Pin 2, both hands are on the same side of the body, so far-side arm attacks, technical mount entries, S-mount entries, and attacks from those positions start to appear. Excellent Pin 2 control usually has strong contact, hip pressure on the chest, and some non-perpendicular orientation: angled toward the face, angled toward the hips, or somewhere between.

Hip-on-chest pressure in Pin 2 is a big part of why the position is so effective. It can give superior control compared with trying to solve everything through shoulder pressure, it puts more pressure on the bottom player, and it gets my arms closer to the far-side arm. This literally means, as the top player, scooting your body forward so that your hip is connecting with the opponent's near-side chest. Initially, this might feel like an off-balanced position, but it has pressure and control advantages compared to being chest-to-chest. The far-side arm becomes the obvious target surface: straight arm locks, kimuras, americanas, and wristlocks all begin to make sense because both of my arms are already on that side of the body.

The straight arm lock, kimura, americana, and wristlock are directly related from here. The difference is where the head-side arm applies control. For the straight arm lock, that control usually sits around the deltoid or the top of the tricep, using the head-side arm. For the kimura, the control shifts down toward the elbow using the head-side arm. Closing that space between my hands lets the kimura grip appear. Kimura or americana aren't working? Give their wrist a lock.

The bottom player's common reaction is to turn away from the top player, toward the trapped far-side arm, and try to withdraw that arm back toward the body. The turn makes distance in the direction that lets the arm retract, space you can claim. They solve the immediate arm problem by creating a back problem. That trade (read: dilemma) is the kind a top player should learn to see. From there, I go directly to side-back control. I shift weight toward the top arm so they cannot easily turn back in, then I take side back. The straight arm lock from Pin 2 is also the most direct side-control example of the idea that every submission starts as a pin. Before the submission finish, the chest, shoulders, deltoid, and arm are all pinned, and the arm is separated, making it difficult to reconnect and easy to break.

Pin 3 has the head-side arm on the far side of the bottom player's body, covering the strike zone. It can be a very effective pin, but the top player is facing away from the opponent. This pin can naturally evolve into a north-south position if necessary. The bottom player can see the top player's back, and the likely escape is to push the top player toward the knees. Because of that, Pin 3 depends heavily on grip discipline: grips behind the back, on the knees, on the hips, or across the waistline that keep the bottom player from using that escape lane freely. The pressure is often oblique into the chest and belly rather than plain chest-to-chest weight.

Pin 3 is also an extension of Pin 2. The bottom player's hands are often on my back pushing me away, or latched to their belt or pants to prevent the far-side arm attack. That defensive attachment can become the next handle, because it creates a hole between their forearm and body. If I thread my near-side arm through that hole from the inside and drive forward, I can create separation and return to the Pin 2 attacking surface. If I go over the arm and bring my arm back through the hole from the outside, I can create separation while catching the wrist, which exposes an immediate wristlock. If I want to retain control and hunt for something less evil than the wristlock (hey, I like my training partners), I thread inside and drive forward.

The hip-side hand in all three pins is the utility and stability hand. It should not be dead. It can post near the inside hip to prevent half-guard recovery, sometimes referred to as a ghost pin. Attached to the near knee, it can push that knee away, turning the spine and exposing the lower back; on the far knee, it can pull toward the top player and turn the spine again. The same hand can cover the hips or waistline, or catch an arm searching for an underhook. It can help turn the bottom player into you for mount, turn them away for side-back control, or simply add another point of spinal misalignment for the sake of mentally breaking your opponent.

The top player should use these positions to either advance or move toward submission. Holding side control forever may win a round under some conditions, but control without progression can become stalling in the student's mind and in the ruleset. Progression can mean submission, but it can also mean mount, knee on belly, back exposure, north-south, stronger isolation, or forcing a predictable escape reaction.

Advancement does not always require leaving side control. You do not have to be chest to chest; you can be hip to chest. Many top players find that awkward or off-balance at first, but for the bottom player it feels like much more pressure than a normal chest-to-chest pin.

The top player's body can be perpendicular and flat, perpendicular but aimed toward the head, perpendicular but aimed toward the feet, off-center instead of perpendicular, or some combination of those angles. Each orientation gives the top player different control and attack options and gives the bottom player different recovery and escape opportunities. Andrew Kerfoot likes to refer to these as configurations and orientations.

## The Bottom Player's Job

Bottom side control survival begins with refusing to panic-push. Extended arms create handles. Straight-arm pushing often gives the top player underhooks, arm isolation, or a path to mount.

The bottom player needs inside frames. A frame is structure placed between the defender and the opponent's pressure (not a bench press) so the defender can turn, breathe, recover angle, and rebuild layers. A forearm in the right place can carry pressure through the skeleton. A hand pushing from a bad angle can exhaust the defender and expose the elbow.

## Hip-Side Frame Placement

Small placement errors in the hip-side frame change the likelihood of successful recovery. The frame should be elbow-to-hip, not forearm-to-hip: a forearm against the hip can look like a frame, but it usually takes very little downward force for the top player to crush it. Elbow-to-hip creates a structural post. Built correctly, it can support the entirety of the top player's weight long enough for the bottom player to pivot, insert the knee, and start unwinding the pass, even if the top player is much larger than you.

Test it yourself: put the bottom player's forearm across the waist with the hand on the hip. Have the top player apply weight. The frame smashes straight to the mat. Now put the bottom player's elbow inside the hip joint and aim the elbow straight up in the air. The bottom player can hold the top player's weight much more easily, even when the bottom player is much smaller.

Top players naturally fight that frame before it becomes useful. The most common counter is for the top player to shift the hips into the frame, or away from it, and either way the frame becomes less useful. Another common answer is to bury the head so the bottom player cannot establish the head-side frame. Another is to shelf the bottom player's inside arm by trapping it between the top player's hip and ribs, making it difficult for the bottom player to get the elbow inside the hip joint. Once the inside elbow is missing, the bottom player has to make distance before they can retract the arm. Without distance, the elbow stays trapped and the recovery is weaker and later.

Head control must be addressed. If the top player turns the face away, the spine follows. The defender may try to shrimp, but the body is not aimed where it needs to go. Clearing or redirecting head pressure often comes before meaningful hip movement.

Knee-elbow connection is one recovery path. Once the bottom player can bring the near knee and elbow back toward each other, guard recovery becomes possible. If that connection is gone, the bottom player may need a different escape route, such as turning in with an underhook, coming to turtle, or timing a reversal when the top player overcommits.

Frames and underhooks both relate to the strike zone. Frames put elbows and forearms between the bodies so the bottom player can make space and then take it. An underhook can go a step further and turn the bottom player's space creation into control over the top player's strike zone, which opens the opportunity to come up on the bottom elbow, enabling wrestling, back-door escapes, reversals, sweeps, and attacks.

A defensive two-on-one can serve the same recovery mindset, but the arm you control changes what you are denying. Control the head-side arm and you can block head control and the spinal misalignment that comes through the neck. Control the hip-side arm and you can block much of the top player's arm-based hip-side control, though they can still use their legs. This configuration of grip is often useful for the bottom player to entangle the top player's hip-side arm between their legs. Hip-side arm control also interferes with attacks that require both arms, which is for the most part all of the side control arm attacks. Single-arm attacks such as papercutter or breadcutter style chokes may still exist.

Neither two-on-one is automatically better. The bottom player has to evaluate the moment: is the top player using that arm for head control, hip control, a two-arm submission, or a path to mount? That read should decide which arm is worth owning.

From bottom, I do not think side control recovery should be taught as one clean sequence. You are going from a bad position to a better one, and jiu jitsu is too dynamic for a single ordered list to answer every pin, angle, and grip.

The bottom player should think about unwinding the pass. If I am flat, how do I get off my back? How do I get things in the way? How do I create space and then take it, even if it is only an inch at a time? Frames are part of that answer, and they are the beginning of recovery if they let me reclaim space on my terms.

The classic making-and-taking-space example from bottom side control is the basic white-belt recovery. Establish frames and push away to make space. Pivot the body away to change the angle, then insert the bottom knee to claim the opening before the top player takes the space back. Without elbow-to-hip structure on the hip-side frame, the recovery often dies before the knee can enter.

The same concept applies when the inside arm has been shelved. If the bottom player retracts that arm, they are making space. Now they have to take it with structure. The exact answer can change: it may mean getting vertical shoulders, reestablishing elbow-to-hip frames, inserting the bottom knee, bringing the knees in, or simply pushing the top person away.

The bottom player's movement is usually trying to do one of a few things: improve position, recover through frames and knees, reverse through underhooks, or compensate for pressure, spinal misalignment, or misplaced knees. If the top player turns the head one way and the knees another, the bottom player will naturally try to realign the spine and knees because the position hurts and weakens them. That is yin and yang again: top misaligns the spine and blocks knees and frames, while bottom tries to realign the spine and insert them.

Two bottom-side mistakes show up constantly. The first is turning away. It is not always wrong, but it is almost always dangerous because it exposes side-back control. When that happens, there is one recovery priority: turn and face the opponent again before the position gets worse. The bottom player usually needs to create space to do that, and the shoulders often become vertical during the turn. Protect that shoulder position. If the top player flattens the shoulders again, the recovery may collapse back into the same pin or become full back exposure. The second mistake is making space without taking it. If you create enough space to insert a frame, recover a knee, or begin an underhook and you do not reclaim that space, the top player will.

When normal escapes and recoveries do not work, my go-to diagnostic is analyzing the top player's weight distribution. The top player cannot be heavy everywhere: if they are heavy on my head and shoulders, they are usually lighter somewhere near the hips, meaning I have leg mobility. If their hips are smothering my stomach and hips, there may be space or movement near the head or through the arms. I usually start with frames. From there, the frames set up an opportunity to make even more space, enable recovery, or open holes to get the underhook. I evaluate heavy and light through the difficulty I have creating those frames and underhooks. The bottom player should identify where the top player is heavy and where they are light, then work the escape or recovery on the light side, which is the path of least resistance.

## Head Height, Hip Height, Grips, and Posture and Base

Head position in side control is a spinal problem. The top player's head and shoulder pressure can make the bottom player face away from the escape. The bottom player must recover enough head freedom to turn.

Hip position determines whether the bottom player can recover guard or create angle. Top hips may float, sprawl, switch, settle, or block depending on the escape threat. A heavy top player with poor base can still be rolled, and a mobile top player who never settles may give the bottom player space. Hip-to-chest pressure can make the bottom player carry a different kind of weight than standard chest-to-chest pressure.

Grips should isolate or rebuild. On top, that means grips that separate elbows, deny frames, and control head position; on bottom, grips that frame, pummel, or open recovery pathways. A grip that only holds cloth or squeezes the opponent closer will likely not solve the problem.

Posture exists underneath too. Both players need structure, of different kinds: the bottom player's posture is frames, spinal alignment, and the ability to turn, and the top player's is pressure connected to base. In side control, top posture changes with the pin: Pin 1, Pin 2, and Pin 3 all use different hand locations, pressure angles, and risks.

## Control Versus Stalling

Side control can become a place where students confuse not losing with winning. The top player may hold tightly without improving while the bottom player survives without rebuilding. Both people feel busy, but the position does not change.

A useful control position should deny the opponent while creating the next step for you. If the top player is controlling head and hips, separating elbows, and forcing reactions, side control is doing work. If the top player is squeezing without isolating or advancing, the control may be more fear than strategy. From bottom, survival is necessary, but survival should be pointed toward structure: frames, angle, knee-elbow connection, and escape.

## Common Misreads

Top players chase submissions before stabilizing, hold tight but never isolate, and forget the hips. Some let the bottom elbow get inside the hip joint and wonder why the bottom knee keeps coming back. Bottom players push with arms instead of building frames and bridge flat without first creating an angle. They ignore head control and wonder why the shrimp feels weak, or they put a forearm on the hip, call it a frame, and watch it collapse.

The top player's two most common side-control mistakes are allowing the bottom player's shoulders to leave the mat, and leaving the hip-side hand unemployed. That hand is a utility. If it is not blocking recovery, catching underhooks, pushing or pulling knees, covering the waistline, or posting to prevent half guard, it is probably wasting an opportunity.

Pushing and pulling the knees turns the bottom player's body. Pull the far knee and the bottom player may turn into you, opening mount. Push the near knee and they turn away, and side-back control can appear. If the head is already turned away and the knees are turned in another direction, the spine becomes heavily contorted. The position becomes uncomfortable, and the bottom player's power drops.

Side control becomes clearer when each person names the opposite job. The top player's job is to keep the bottom player flat, misaligned, framed-out, and late; the bottom player's job is to get to a side, align the spine, frame inside, reconnect, and move.

## Training Observation

From bottom side control, ask three questions before choosing an escape: can I turn my head, can I turn my hips, and can I connect my knee and elbow? From top, ask which of those three you are denying.

Add a pin-identification round. The top player starts in Pin 1, Pin 2, or Pin 3 and names the pin before moving. The bottom player names the likely escape lane before trying to recover. After the reset, both players say where the top player was heavy, where they were light, and whether the hip-side hand had a job.

Add a hip-side frame round. The bottom player may not recover guard until the hip-side frame is elbow-to-hip. The top player tries to bury the head or shelf the inside arm before the frame becomes structural. After each reset, both players identify whether the frame was elbow-to-hip or only forearm-to-hip.
