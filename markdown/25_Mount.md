# Mount

Mount occurs when the top player sits over the opponent's torso with both legs outside the opponent's legs, generally facing the opponent's head. Students often learn it as a dominant position, which is true, but mount is a progression rather than one static pin.

The top player may begin low (toward the hips) to stabilize, climb higher to isolate arms, shift to technical mount as the defender turns, or move between pressure, hand fighting, submissions, and back exposure. The bottom player is trying to interrupt that progression by protecting the neck, keeping elbows connected, creating angle, and recovering enough structure to escape. In general, the attacker wants to advance higher, and the defender wants them to retreat to create space.

Mount becomes dominant when the top player separates knees from elbows, elbows from ribs, and arms from posture. The bottom player survives and escapes by reconnecting those structures.

## The Top Player's Job

Stabilize before attacking. Many students lose mount because they see a neck, elbows, or exposed arms and rush to advance. The defender bridges, frames, turns, or recovers half guard because the top player skipped the work of control.

Low mount helps settle weight and prevent easy hip movement. High mount separates elbows and exposes arms, and technical mount can follow the defender's turn to threaten the back or arm. None of those versions is automatically best, so the top player's hip position should respond to the bottom player's defensive reaction.

Climbing from low to high mount is its own small battle, and it is won with the legs more than the hands. There are two ways up: elevate the defender's elbows, or jam the knees underneath and wedge upward. I prefer to let the legs do the work. If you drive forcefully with the knees, the defender's elbows get forced up off the ribs, often right into your lap, and an elbow in your lap is an arm that is ready for full exposure, so the climb itself delivers the arm.

The top player wants the defender's arms to become liabilities, so an elbow that was protecting the ribs gets walked higher and a hand that was protecting the neck gets separated. A frame becomes an armbar entry and a turn becomes back exposure, which is why the top player should climb through the defender's reactions instead of forcing every attack.

High mount gives a clean obstacle-removal example. From top high mount, I usually have two objectives: expose the neck or expose the elbows. Exposing the neck means getting things out of the way so a hand can enter the collar or a choking path can open. Exposing the elbows means moving parts of the arm out of the way or getting underneath them. The bottom player is trying to put those things back in the way.

The strongest dilemma from there is usually armbar and triangle, or the progression into S-mount and armbar. The defender needs hands and elbows to protect the neck, but those same reactions can expose the arm. If they overcommit to hiding the arm, the neck and triangle angles can start to appear. The wristlock is always there too, a constant threat in the room, but it is not the main story of mount.

Gravity should help. A tense mount that squeezes hard with the legs feels secure but can become light in the wrong places, while a connected mount makes the defender carry the position with hip pressure, head position, weight distribution, hand fighting, and grapevines or feet when appropriate.

The armbar is the clearest mount attack for showing progressive isolation. It can be taught as a long chain of cutting your opponent in half: closed guard to half guard, half guard to side control, side control through knee on belly, knee on belly to mount, mount to high mount, high mount to S-mount, and then armbar.

The final armbar matters less than the narrowing that takes place on the way there. Guard is body against body, and passing to half guard changes that relationship before side control cuts the defender in half again. Knee on belly forces reactions. Mount begins separating elbows, high mount leaves the arms weaker and more vulnerable, and S-mount turns one arm into the problem. By the time the armbar appears, a large part of the attacker's body is focused on a smaller and smaller part of the defender's body.

## The Bottom Player's Job

The bottom player must protect elbows and neck before chasing escape. Panic pushing is one of the fastest ways to lose the mount battle, because straight arms give the top player the separation they need. A bridge without angle may lift the top player for a moment and return them to a better mount.

Reconnection is the defensive theme. Elbows return to ribs, knees toward elbows, and hands to neck protection, and the hips connect to a bridge or shrimp only after the upper body has a job. The bottom player needs to be organized, not comfortable.

A defensive two-on-one can be part of that organization, but it is hard to create a full escape path from a two-on-one under mount, unless the two-on-one is against a hip. It is much easier to create a blocker against the top player's attacks. I usually use it to protect myself while preparing to move one or both hands to a hip or knee and begin an escape or recovery process. If the top player needs an arm to climb, isolate the elbow, or open the neck, two controls on that arm can slow the attack long enough for the bottom player to reconnect, bridge, turn, or recover a safer layer.

Escapes change based on the top player's mount. Low mount may ask for hip movement, frames, and guard recovery, since the top player is directly above the most powerful center of the bottom player. High mount may instead ask for elbow recovery and patience before the arms become isolated, and technical mount for back-defense awareness and shoulder position. A strong bridge may be available if a post is trapped and the angle is real; a shrimp, if space and knee-elbow connection return.

Whichever mount you are under, do not stay flat. A flat back is a heavy back, and a heavy bottom player is a pinned bottom player. Turning to a side is the key to almost every real mount escape, because a side gives you a bridge with direction, a shoulder to frame with, and a hip that can move. The cost is that turning flirts with back exposure, and that risk is real. Even so, do not lie flat where it feels safe, because flat is neither safe nor productive. Turn with a plan.

That plan can even use the top player's climb against them. When the top player commits to technical mount or S-mount, the elbow becomes a wedge. Open enough space to bring both arms through, and the same climb that was meant to isolate an arm can let the bottom player slip out the back door as the top player rises. If the back door is not there, use the side position to reconnect the legs instead. Clear the arms to one side, bridge the hips to off-balance the top player, and slip a knee underneath their leg to recover half guard or single-leg X. Flat is the trap. The side is the escape.

The defensive mindset under mount should be calm enough not to give away arms and urgent enough not to let the top player climb without resistance.

## The Height Dilemma

Mount rewards one clean read: where are the top player's hips? Almost every low-mount escape depends on the top player being low, down near the defender's hips. The bridge and roll, which most rooms call the upa, needs the top player's weight forward over the hips so the bridge has something to throw. The elbow-knee escape needs room near the hips to insert a knee and shrimp. The first two escapes a white belt learns both live in low mount.

That shared weakness is why a good top player climbs. One move up the body defeats both escapes at once. When the top player advances to high mount, up around the chest and armpits, the bridge and roll loses its target, because there are no longer hips underneath to tip, and the elbow-knee escape loses its room, because the top player is too high to let a knee slide in. This is also why the counters to those escapes are about defending the direction the bottom player commits to. Against the bridge and roll, widen the base: flare the knees, post the hands wide, and if they try to roll to one side, post the knee or foot wide on that exact side to block the rotation, while sinking the hips into the belt line so they cannot generate an explosive arch. When the roll fails against that wide base, the failed attempt is itself the opening, and I usually prefer to use that moment to dismount cleanly into side control rather than grind to stay on top. Against the elbow-knee escape, drop the hips heavy, keep the heels tight to the defender's tailbone so the legs cannot flatten and slip under, and punch an underhook or move straight to technical mount to shut the hips down.

High mount is not free, though, and this is the yin and yang of the position. By climbing off the hips, the top player hands the bottom player's legs back to them. From underneath a high mount, the bottom player can begin hunting leg entanglements, trapping a leg to make space, pulling it in, and starting a guard recovery. Climbing also opens the back door covered above. So mount is a height argument: low mount owns the legs and concedes the bridge and the shrimp, while high mount kills both of those and concedes the legs and the back. The bottom player should run the escape that matches the height they are actually under, not the escape they memorized. That is the Konami Code habit (refer back to Konami Code) of reading which directions are real from here before choosing one.

## Head Height, Hip Height, Grips, and Posture and Base

Head position affects pressure, hand fighting, and neck safety. The top player may use the head to post, pressure, hide attack entries, or control the defender's line of sight. The bottom player's head position affects breathing and the ability to bridge, and a head turned the wrong way can weaken the escape.

The top player's hip position defines the mount. Low mount, high mount, and technical mount have different advantages, disadvantages, offensive opportunities, and defensive openings. The top hips decide whether the position is stable, climbing, attacking, or following. The bottom hips create bridges and angles, but only when connected to useful upper-body structure.

Grips under mount are mostly about isolation and exposure from the top and protection from the bottom. The top player grips to separate arms, control the head, deny frames, and force reactions, while the bottom player grips to protect the neck, trap posts, and recover elbow position. A bottom grip that pulls the top player closer without improving structure may only increase pressure.

Base belongs to the top player, who must maintain it while climbing, but posture belongs to both, and the bottom player must create it from underneath through frames, angle, and timing.

## Common Misreads

On top, players squeeze the legs instead of controlling alignment, attack without stabilizing, or stay low forever and never separate the elbows. On bottom, they push upward with extended arms, let high mount happen and try to solve it too late, or bridge flat and wonder why the top player floats back into place.

Mount becomes easier to study if you watch the elbows, which separate when the top player is winning and reconnect when the bottom player is recovering.

## Training Observation

In mount rounds, track only elbow position for the first minute. Top player: can you separate one elbow without losing base? Bottom player: can you reconnect one elbow without exposing the neck or back?
