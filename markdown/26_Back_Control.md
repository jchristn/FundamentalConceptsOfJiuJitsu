# Back Control

Back control is the most one-sided position in jiu jitsu because of geometry. When you are behind someone, you can see them and they cannot see you, and your hands, chest, hips, and legs are all pointed at a target that cannot point anything back. Everything the position gives you, and everything that makes it frightening from underneath, comes from one player having been put behind the other player's ability to face them.

That asymmetry also tells both players their job, and almost everything either of them does on the back serves that job. The attacker keeps the defender from turning back in and uses the free time it buys to hunt the neck or an arm. The defender protects the neck long enough to win back the hands, square the shoulders, and turn to face the fight again.

The finish comes at the end of that process. Everyone pictures the choke from the back, but the position also feeds arm attacks and triangles. Whatever the finish, an attacker who reaches for it before owning the position is usually just squeezing, and a defender who fights it while ignoring the position is only delaying it.

## The Attacker's Job

Keep them from facing you. Hooks, a body triangle, a hip clamp, the seatbelt, head position, and shoulder pressure are all ways of doing that, and none of them matters for its own sake. The moment the defender wins alignment and turns in, the back is gone no matter how good the grip looked a second before.

Because the neck is guarded by hands, the fight for the neck starts as a fight for the hands. A choke thrown at a disciplined pair of hands turns into a squeeze that goes nowhere, so before the finish the attacker is separating a defensive hand from the neck, trapping an arm, occupying one hand while the other works, or building a threat real enough that the defender has to choose between defending a hook and defending the neck and cannot do both. Head position is part of the same job: if your head keeps the defender's head from turning, their shoulders and hips are late to follow, and late is all you need.

None of this commits you to a choke. The back is a control position that can become a choke, an arm attack, a mount, or a better level of back control, and treating the back as only a choke throws away most of what the position offers. I teach it as a hub on purpose, with chokes from both the top-side and the bottom-side arm, arm-in chokes, and T-kimura transitions into armbars, kimuras, and triangles. When the defender falls to a side, clears a hook, or turns in, that is a different branch, not a failure.

The lower body is where the live choice sits, and each of the three common controls gives something up to get something else. Two hooks keep you mobile. Each leg steers on its own, so you can follow a scrambling opponent, switch sides, and pull one hook out to trap an arm; because nothing is locked, you have to keep reading which hook is under threat. A body triangle keeps the opponent confined, with the legs forming a closed loop around the hips that holds an explosive opponent while you hand fight. The legs can no longer move independently, though, and the locking foot becomes something the opponent can hunt. Post control, one hook in place while the other lower leg slides under the far leg and braces foot to foot, gives you direction and access. It rotates the opponent and opens the space to free an arm, but it points one way and stops helping the moment the opponent changes theirs. This is the same tradeoff that runs through side control.

A shorthand for the three: hooks are steering, the body triangle is a parking brake, and post control is a wedge. The control should follow the objective. Steer with the hooks to follow an opponent while the position is still changing, set the brake to hold once you have the orientation you want and plan to hand fight for the finish, and use the wedge to open and isolate when you need to rotate the opponent or pry an arm loose. None of the three is strongest in the abstract, and none of them rescues a back that has already lost the battle above the waist. If the opponent gets a shoulder blade to the mat and separates your chest from their back, even a perfect lower-body lock only keeps you attached while they leave.

## When the Back Is Leaving

The attacker's hardest decision on the back is knowing when to stop chasing the choke and convert the position into something else. The read is a change in the kind of connection you feel, more than just a single stripped hook or a shoulder grazing the mat. Healthy back control is compressive. The defender is held between your chest and your legs, the seatbelt is quiet, and your hands are free to attack because your torso and legs are doing the controlling. Dying back control is tensile. The seatbelt has become a strap you are hanging from, your elbows are extending, and your arms are the only thing still keeping the defender from turning. Do not use your arms to preserve a back that your chest and legs have already lost.

That gives a cleaner test than watching any one limb. Could you loosen the seatbelt slightly and still stay behind them? If yes, the back is healthy, so keep hand fighting and finishing. If the honest answer is that they would immediately turn and face you, the seatbelt is only hiding a position that has already gone, and it is time to reclaim your back control or abandon it.

Read the thresholds in two colors. On top, a shoulder touching the mat is yellow, and the broad shoulder blade passing your sternum is red. Below, a stripped hook is yellow, and a hip escaping outside your knee line is red. One yellow is a warning to fix the position. Red on either connection means convert. The best moment to convert is the window when the defender has committed to the escape but has not finished it, because their movement is predictable, their limbs are often extended, and their hips may still be yours. Convert too early and you throw away good back control. Convert too late and you are being towed by the seatbelt while they turn into your guard and end up on top. Transition at the right time and you can confidently land in mount. Even landing in mount, though, is a step down from back control, because your opponent is now facing the threat.

Where you convert follows the escape, not a favorite finish. If the defender wins the shoulder line while their hips are still between your legs, climb to technical mount and then to mount as they flatten. At that point they may turn, but they will turn underneath you. If their hips escape while you are still attached above, follow them into turtle (see the Turtle chapter) or a riding control instead of forcing a finish from the side. If the choke is already finished, keep it, because a locked choke can outlive the position. The dividing question is whether the neck is already captured or you are still trying to capture it. Do not leap from a dying back to a hopeful armbar. Convert to an arm only when the arm is already isolated, trapped and off the ribs, because visibility is not isolation.

The compact version is the one to keep: chest and legs hold the back, arms start and finish the attack, and when the arms have to hold back control because your chest and legs aren't, your back control is leaving.

## The Defender's Job

Underneath, the problem is that same asymmetry, and the defender chips away at it in a specific order. The neck comes first, because losing the neck ends the conversation before anything else matters. The hands are next, since the neck is only as safe as the hands protecting it, and only then do the shoulders and hips go to work, because they are what turns a lost position back into an even one. Rushing the order is how defenders escape their hips straight into a choke.

Protecting the neck takes layers: chin down, hands inside, shoulders working, all buying the time it takes to turn. One hand fighting two attacking hands does not last long, so inside hand position is the first thing worth winning. When the choking arm is the immediate danger, a two-on-one on that arm belongs here too, but only for what it is. The attacker needs only one arm to finish, so a two-on-one on the back is an emergency measure that says "I am not getting choked in this exact moment." It is not the escape. It keeps the choking arm from becoming final while the shoulders and hips go to work.

That work often starts before the back is fully lost: if a bad turn in side control has only exposed side back, the first priority is just to turn and face the opponent again, which usually needs space and a live shoulder line. The shoulders may come briefly vertical during the turn, and they have to be kept from going flat. Once full back control is set, the problem hardens into neck safety, hand position, shoulder line, hip movement, and hook control, and the hips have to move with a plan instead of in a panic: down toward the attacker's lap, up toward the head when that is the better route, or flat to the mat to start a guard recovery or a scramble to top.

Which way to turn is really two questions that defenders wrongly treat as one. The head and the body do not turn the same way. At the neck, turn the jaw toward the choking arm, not away from it. Turning the face away feels natural, and it is basically suicide. It opens the side of the neck and lets the jaw travel over the forearm, so the attacker finishes without moving the arm as far. Turning the jaw toward the elbow keeps the chin connected to the shoulder and denies the arm its path. Below the neck, the shoulders and hips escape the other way, toward wherever the attacker's alignment is weakest, which is often the side away from the choking arm. One motion protects the neck and the other attacks the position, so it is fine that they can point in opposite directions.

The wrong direction is less about left or right than about any turn that improves the attacker's choke without breaking their chest-to-back or hip-to-hip connection. If the defender rotates and the attacker just rides along, the defender spent energy and changed nothing. When the defender rotates and the attacker's sternum comes off their spine, the back is starting to leave.

The one cue to keep under the stress of a back attack is to replace their chest with the floor. Get the flat of a shoulder blade to the mat, not just the point of the shoulder, then get the hip outside the attacker's knee line. The shoulder blade breaks the upper connection and the hip breaks the lower one. If you win only the shoulder, the attacker can still ride into mount; if you win only the hip, the seatbelt and the choking arm may still be there. The escape completes when both connections break, which is the same picture the attacker sees as the back leaving, read from the other side.

## Head Height, Hip Height, Grips, and Posture and Base

The four battles still run here in back-control form. Head height becomes the fight over turning: the attacker's head pins the defender's from following the escape, and the defender's head protects the neck without freezing the shoulders. Hip height is the fight the hooks and the body triangle are having, managing the defender's rotation while the defender hunts enough freedom to clear a hook or slide the shoulders toward the mat. Grips are the loudest battle of the four, because back control is hand fighting with consequences, the attacker prying defensive hands off the neck while the defender rebuilds inside layers faster than they are stripped. Posture and base still exist, only turned inside out: the attacker needs posture that stays connected through rolls and turns, and the defender rebuilds posture without ever trading the neck to get it.

All four battles here are fought over one relationship, which is why the back is the clearest example of centerline denial in the book. The back is a state in which one player sits behind the other player's ability to face them, and every grip, hook, and head position is spent either holding that state or breaking it. Lose your centerline and you hand someone the way in; win the hand fight and the shoulder line and you take it back.

## Common Misreads

Almost every back-control mistake is an order-of-operations error: someone is fighting the finish when the position mattered more, or the position when the finish mattered more. On top, the attacker reaches for the choke before winning the hands, squeezes from an angle that is already lost, or hangs on from a dying position instead of converting while the escape is still half finished. Underneath, the defender clears a hook while leaving the neck open, accepts hand separation and then tries to escape on hips alone, or turns the wrong way because they never sorted out which side the choking arm is on.

The exchange gets far more readable when both players track only three things: neck safety, hand position, and shoulder line. Almost all progress, in either direction, shows up in those three before any choke or escape does.

## Training Observation

For thirty seconds of a back-control round, ignore the choke entirely. The attacker tries only to separate one defensive hand while keeping shoulder control. The defender tries only to keep both hands useful while walking one shoulder closer to alignment.
