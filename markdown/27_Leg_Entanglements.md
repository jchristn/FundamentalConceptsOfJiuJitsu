# Leg Entanglements

Leg entanglements are positions where one or both players use their legs to control the opponent's legs, hips, posture, or balance. The category includes guard structures such as De la Riva, reverse De la Riva, X-guard, single-leg X, 50/50, saddle-style positions, and many related entries and exits (refer back to Open Guard).

The subject can become confusing quickly because the legs are doing many jobs at once. A hook may off-balance while a clamp controls the knee line, a foot position blocks the hip, and a grip breaks posture. A small angle change may then turn a sweep position into a submission threat, a back exposure, or a passing opportunity.

The leg attacks are only part of it. Both players are also fighting over hip access, knee-line control, rotational control, posture, and the ability to stand, turn, or free the trapped limb.

Leg entanglements are an exciting place to train, and some of the greatest grapplers alive have gone so deep into them that it is not even funny. My job is not to be the best in the world at leg locks, heel hooks, kneebars, 50/50, or other entanglements and submissions. It is to understand and convey the core concepts a student needs to be aware of. So this chapter stays focused on the two primary attacks that need real attention: the basic straight ankle lock and the basic heel hook. I will mention inside versus outside heel-hook control, backside 50/50, and other positions, but the main job is conceptual: how the attacker controls, how the defender identifies danger, and why and when the position becomes safe or unsafe.

## Why the Legs Are So Dangerous

Leg entanglements are effective for two reasons, and both are worth understanding before any specific attack. The first is the sheer amount of control. A good entanglement usually engages three points at once: two with your legs and at least one with an arm. Most positions hand you two points of contact to fight with. Here you have three, all converging on a single limb, which is why a smaller person can pin a much larger one and keep them there.

The second reason is where the attack lands. A leg lock attacks a joint that sits about as far as possible from the opponent's primary defensive tools, which are the hands. In an armbar or a choke, the defender's hands are right in the fight, close to the danger, working for the grip. Down at the ankle and the knee, the hands are far away and slow to arrive. By the time they get there, the structure is often already built.

Those two reasons also make leg entanglements a clear example of a rule that runs through the whole book: every submission starts as a pin. Before I finish anything, whether it is an armbar, a kimura, or a leg entanglement leading to a straight ankle lock or a heel hook, I want my opponent secured and unable to back out of what I have put them in without a tremendous amount of effort. That is what I mean by a pin, and the finish comes only after it holds.

In a leg entanglement the pin has a specific structure. It usually starts with one leg underneath the leg you are attacking, the heel tucked tightly into the hip bone. That is the bottom leg, and it is the anchor the rest of the entanglement is built on. The other leg, usually the top leg, wants a strong connection from the knee to the heel attached to the hip bone, so the whole structure clamps onto the opponent's hip. From there you squeeze and pull, keeping the thigh isolated and immobilized. That is two of the three points of control accounted for with the legs alone. The third point is the position of the arm you are going to attack with, which is where the specific finish begins.

## The Entangling Player's Job

The entangling player needs to identify what line they are controlling. The goal may be the hip line, the knee line, posture, a foot, an ankle, or the opponent's ability to step, backstep, turn, or stand.

Off-balancing should usually come before attack, because a standing passer with strong posture and free hips is hard to sweep or leg lock. Once that passer is tilted, forced to post, made to carry weight, or prevented from stepping freely, the entanglement becomes more powerful. Whether the legs are acting as hooks, frames, wedges, clamps, or levers, they need direction.

The entangling player should also know what the position is trying to become. A straight ankle lock asks whether the attacker controls the leg well enough to apply force through the foot and ankle while denying the defender's ability to clear, turn, or stand. A heel hook asks a more serious rotational question: where is the knee line, where is the hip, which direction can the defender safely turn, and does the defender understand the danger before force appears?

Other guards and entanglements can still appear as context. De la Riva may be a guard form, a sweep platform, a back-take threat, or a way to expose posture, while X-guard may be a way to elevate and disrupt base. Single-leg X may connect to sweeps, standing entries, or a pathway into leg attacks. What 50/50 becomes depends on rules and skill: it may be a control, sweep, stall, or submission position. A leg entanglement without a purpose can become a knot both players are waiting to untie.

## The Disentangling Player's Job

The defender's first task is to name what is trapped, and the foot being touched is not always the main problem. The knee line may be trapped, the hip blocked, the posture broken, or the ability to turn controlled. The foot may be safe while the base is compromised, or the base may feel stable while the knee line is in danger.

When leg locks are present, knee-line awareness becomes urgent. A defender who frees the foot but leaves the knee trapped may not have escaped the real danger. Spinning without understanding the direction of rotational force may make the problem worse, and defenders can actually submit or break themselves that way. Trying to stand before removing the hook, wedge, or clamp may hand the entangling player a very powerful sweep.

Instead of moving in a panic, remove the controls in order: posture, grips, hooks, knee line, hip mobility, base, and direction. The order changes by position, but the habit of naming the control should stay the same.

## Straight Ankle Locks, Heel Hooks, and Related Positions

The straight ankle lock is the natural place to begin because the danger is easier to explain and easier to feel than many rotational attacks. The attacker has to control the leg, manage the defender's ability to stand or turn, and apply force through the foot and ankle with structure. The defender has to know whether the knee line is free, whether the hip can move, and whether posture or base has already been compromised.

A lot of people miss the detail that decides whether this finish is real. A straight ankle lock is a lot like a guillotine, and like a guillotine it lives or dies on the angle of the forearm. Once the pin is set, look at the line your forearm cuts across the back of the leg, across the Achilles. Most people are up on an elbow, and from there the forearm crosses the Achilles on a diagonal line. A diagonal line is not optimal, and it is why so many ankle locks feel close but never finish.

The blade you want is vertical, square across the Achilles. The way to move from diagonal to vertical is to use the free hand, in a gable grip or a pushing C-grip, to drive the attacking hand up toward the top of your chest while you roll off the bottom elbow onto your side. That roll turns the diagonal into a vertical slice on its own. When the roll is done, your attacking arm's hand should point toward your chin. Then, with the pin still holding, you extend yourself back while pulling your arms into your chest through your lat muscles, which slides the attacking arm down toward the heel and applies the finish. The ankle lock depends on the angle of the blade and the structure you finish it from, and squeezing harder does not make up for either.

The defense runs the same logic in reverse, and it also has an order. The most common defense you will meet is the defender pushing the attacked foot off the hip and covering it with their tailbone, sitting on it. That alone neutralizes the lock, because the pin has lost its anchor. If they cannot get the foot off the hip, you will often see them shove the foot under attack farther through the hold, past you, so the ankle ends up behind the attacker. That can work, but only if they finish the thought. As the attacker, if you keep the pin, you can reset the position, holding the pin with your heel and knees, and push backward to adjust the grip back to the ankle. So the defender who shoves the foot through has to also grip your upper body, and pull you in, close enough that you cannot retract back toward the heel, or pull themselves up on the attacker's backward motion with the safe foot on the ground.

My coach Andrew at Nexus put the whole defense in one line: first beat the foot; if you cannot beat the foot, beat the knee; if you cannot beat the knee, beat the grips and close the distance to the upper body. It is a cascade. Each step is what you reach for when the one before it is gone, and the last step, closing the distance, is the one people forget, which is how they clear the foot and still get caught.

The heel hook deserves attention because it teaches a different kind of danger. Inside control, outside control, backside 50/50, and related positions all change the direction of force and the defender's safe responses. I will not catalogue every variation. I want you to respect the underlying questions: what controls the knee line, what controls the hip, where rotation is available, and when movement becomes unsafe.

What makes the heel hook a different animal is the warning, or the lack of one. A straight ankle lock involves pain, and the pain tells you to tap while there is still time before the break. A heel hook does not. It does you no favors. It will break long before you feel pain, and there is not much pain even then. What you get instead is a pop, a strong sense of pulsating pressure and tension, and then the moment you stand you know something is wrong. That is usually about when you realize you are going to be taking more than a few months off the mat. The reason is that the attack is really on the knee, not the heel, and the knee gives no useful warning under rotation. This is the whole reason heel hooks and similar submissions should be trained differently from other submissions. The defender cannot count on pain to save them, so recognition has to happen earlier, before the force is ever there, and often by the attacker. In the training room, it is the attacker's responsibility to keep the defending opponent safe.

Because pain is not the signal, the first and best defense is not being caught in the first place. Most heel-hook defense is early defense, and it follows the same cascade Andrew gave for the ankle lock: beat the foot, then the knee, then the grips. Keep the body moving in and out so the entanglement never settles. Fight the attacker's hands before they capture and isolate your heel, because a heel they cannot grip is a heel they cannot attack. Clear the knee line, since the rotation only reaches the knee when the knee is trapped, and a freed knee takes the torque out of the position. Work a foot to the floor for a base and a post. And if you are already turning, roll in the direction that unwinds the rotation rather than feeding it, because rolling the wrong way heel hooks you on your own momentum.

A couple of habits make that early defense more reliable. Connect your own feet or knees together before the attacker can isolate and get under your heel, because if they cannot get under the heel, there is no heel to attack. And if the rotation is already turning you, turn with it, toward the trouble rather than away from it, so your knee chases the heel and the joint stays aligned instead of lagging behind the force that breaks it.

There is a matching lesson for the attacker. The moment you lose the pin, you have a decision: reacquire it, or read the loss as a signal to advance another way. A common example is losing the connection of the bottom foot to the hip. The defender is often still fairly immobile, and you still control their leg, which makes it easy to climb up on top of them instead of chasing the finish you just lost. The leg you still hold is a handle, and a controlled leg steers the whole body; think of it as a leg drag. The entanglement was a control position before it was ever a submission, so even with the finish gone, that handle lets you come up and pass, or off-balance them into a sweep.

De la Riva, reverse De la Riva, X-guard, single-leg X, 50/50, and saddle-style positions show different versions of leg control: outside hooks, inside frames, and elevation, along with hip access, knee-line control, rotational control, and base disruption.

## Head Height, Hip Height, Grips, and Posture and Base

Head height affects posture, balance, and the ability to stand or come up. In many leg entanglements, breaking posture makes the legs more vulnerable because the defender cannot freely step, turn, or clear lines.

Hip access is central. The player who controls distance to the hips often controls whether the exchange becomes a sweep, pass, back exposure, or submission threat. A defender with free hips has options, while one whose hips are pinned or extended may be solving the attack too late.

Grips often combine upper-body control with lower-body hooks. A foot hook without posture control may be temporary, and a strong grip without hip control may still fail, so the connection you want is the one that makes standing, turning, posting, or clearing more difficult.

Base is unstable in leg entanglements because posts are constantly being lifted, trapped, or redirected. The standing or kneeling player must maintain base without exposing the knee line. The entangling player must create imbalance before expecting control to hold.

## Safety and Rules

Leg locks require unusually clear training standards. Some attacks create late pain feedback. Rotational force can damage a joint before the defender feels the same warning they might feel in a straight arm lock. Skill level, academy rules, tournament rules, age, belt rank, and partner trust all matter.

Controlled mechanics, slow application, early tapping, and honest communication are required parts of the technique. A room that trains leg entanglements responsibly can study them deeply; a room that treats them like surprise attacks teaches fear instead of understanding.

Control should be taught first, as its own skill. That is true even with white belts. A newer student can learn where the danger is without being encouraged to rip through the finish. Establish the control. Explain the mechanics. In the gym, catch and release is often the right practice, above all around the knee and heel.

I do not use strict rank boundaries for most of this material because rank is not the real safety mechanism. Understanding is. A white belt who understands why a heel hook is dangerous can safely learn the basics of entering entanglements, recognizing attacking systems, and defending with low risk. The line in the room is whether the student understands the danger and trains it with control, not whether a white belt is allowed to see the position.

In live training, the standard is catch and release first. Establish the position, recognize the control, then move on. With more experienced students, even black belts, the finish should still be applied slowly and methodically enough that the defender has more than enough time to understand the danger. If they do not recognize that they are already in trouble, go back to catch and release. Walking out of class without a new injury is a win, and each student should want the same thing for their partners.

## Common Misreads

The entangling player attacks the foot without controlling the knee line or hip. The defender spins without understanding the direction of danger. A player treats De la Riva or X-guard as a fixed position instead of a dynamic control system. The passer tries to step out before removing the hook or wedge that controls the leg. The guard player holds a beautiful entanglement but never off-balances.

Leg entanglements become less mysterious when you ask what line is controlled: foot, knee, hip, spine, posture, or base. Then ask what must be freed first.

## Training Observation

In leg entanglement rounds, pause the urge to name the position. Ask what is trapped and what is free. Knee line, hip line, posture, base, and rotation will usually tell you more than the label.
