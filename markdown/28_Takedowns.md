# Takedowns and the Standing Battle

Most matches start on the feet, so most matches start here. This chapter is not a wrestling or judo manual, and it is not trying to make anyone a takedown specialist. As everywhere else in the book, I want you to understand what is happening in the exchange, so you can get the fight to the ground on your terms without getting hurt or handing the position away.

The standing phase is where the four battles first appear. Head height, hip height, grips, and posture and base are all being fought before anyone touches the mat, and the player who wins them standing usually decides whether the match becomes their guard, their pass, or their pin. Getting the opponent down safely is its own skill, and the room's safety standard governs it: no slams, no jumping guard, and the goal of putting someone on the mat without injuring them (the Rules and Safety Note has the details).

On the feet, those battles run primarily on grips. I use grips to do one of two things: establish control over the opponent's strike zone, i.e. their waist and hips, or entangle their legs, either with my arms or with my own legs. Once I have that control, the takedown is usually driving force or pressure applied to off-balance them, taking them to the mat. The grip and the off-balance are the position and the throw or the shot is the finish, the same way a pin comes before a submission everywhere else in the book.

## The Standing Version of the Four Battles

All four matter just as much standing as they do on the ground. Grips tend to lead, because grips are how I start to control posture and the strike zone before anyone changes levels. The battle that usually decides the takedown is the one over posture and base. If I can affect the opponent's posture to a greater degree than they can affect mine, the off-balance is available and the takedown is close. If I cannot, forcing a shot usually hands the exchange to the other player.

## Getting Them Down Without Getting Hurt

Takedowns are the single place people get hurt most. A bad landing can catch a knee, a back, or a neck, and even a wrist, since someone can wrist lock themselves in a scramble just by posting wrong on the way down, whether they are taking the shot or defending it. So the standing game must carry the room's safety standard with it. No slams, and no jumping guard. The goal of a takedown in training is to put the other person on the mat without hurting them, not to drive them into it. A throw you cannot control is not a takedown you have earned yet, because controlling the opponent as they go down is what keeps the landing safe for both people. Examined through another lens, if you cannot control the way a takedown lands, you will have difficulty landing in an advantaged position.

I treat this the way I treat leg locks. In the gym, secure the takedown position and then execute it slowly and methodically, without much explosive force. It is the same restraint I ask for around a heel hook, though here it is usually secure the position and execute slowly rather than pure catch and release. The place for aggression and explosive movement is not the training room, unless the class is explicitly a competition class, and even then, be smart about how much force you apply. We all have jobs to get to, families to go home to, and lives outside jiu jitsu. You want your training partners to go home healthy so they can come back tomorrow and make you better, the same way they should want that for you. The room's full standard lives in the Rules and Safety Note appendix.

## Grips, Ties, and Inside Position

Standing grip fighting is the same problem as inside position on the ground. The hands that get inside and control the strike zone get to decide what happens next, and the hands that end up outside are usually reacting. A grip is worth attacking from if it controls the opponent's posture or their strike zone, and holding cloth does neither. When two players hand fight, they are deciding who gets to off-balance whom, and a two-on-one on a single arm settles that question standing just as it does on the mat. As a general rule of thumb, in grip fighting, if you find your opponent has a more dominant grip than you, you need to first address the grips before addressing anything else. Get dominant grips.

## Shooting, Throwing, and Pulling as Choices

When I am standing across from someone, the choice among shooting, throwing, pulling guard, and continuing to hand fight is a read, not a habit. I always ask first: do I have dominant grip position, and can I affect their posture to a greater degree than they can affect mine? If the answer is no, my job is to keep hand fighting until it becomes yes. If the answer is yes, the opponent's posture tells me which attack is there. Is their head height low or high? Are their elbows tucked and covering their strike zone, or are their arms up and out? Is their stance square or staggered? Each of those changes whether the next move is a shot, a throw, a guard pull, or more hand fighting. I read what their posture has left open the same way I read a dilemma on the ground.

I usually do not pull guard, and that is a preference with a reason behind it. I agree with Chris Haueter's rules: be the guy on top, win on top, and stay on top; when you are on bottom, have a guard they shall not pass; and never fall in love with your guard, because it makes you lazy. To me, pulling guard is an admission that I am okay being underneath somebody, and I would rather earn the top position than concede the bottom one. That is a personal stance. There are a lot of really good guard players out there, and I do not want to discount them in any way. It is not how I choose to start.

When I teach the standing game, I keep it to fundamentals: stance, bent knees, inside position control, and dominant grips. I want control of my own strike zone so I can mitigate their ability to control mine. I want the knees bent so I can drop and sprawl quickly if they shoot. All of it goes toward one habit, which is to always be the guy on top.

## Common Misreads

The most common standing mistake is shooting or throwing before winning the grip and posture battle. If I cannot affect the opponent's posture more than they can affect mine, the attack is a hope, and a failed shot often hands them the better position. Another is pulling guard out of habit, conceding the bottom before the standing exchange has even been decided. A third is holding cloth without using the grip for control, so the hands are busy while the opponent's posture and strike zone stay free. The most dangerous misread is bringing competition-level aggression into an ordinary training round, because explosive force on the feet is how partners get hurt.

## Training Observation

In standing rounds, do not start by naming a takedown. Watch the grip and posture battle first. Who has dominant grips, and how did the other player respond? Who has inside control? Whose posture is being affected more than they are affecting the other? Who controls their own strike zone, and who is reaching? The player winning those questions is usually the one about to score the takedown, whether or not either player has committed to a shot yet.
