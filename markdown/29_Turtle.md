# Turtle

Turtle is the position a player reaches when they are down on their hands and knees, folded over their own space, denying the opponent the back and the hips while giving up the ability to face them. It shows up after a stuffed takedown, a failed guard recovery, a scramble, or a deliberate choice to shell up and reset. Many players treat it as a place to hide, but that is only half of what it is.

Turtle is best understood through yin and yang (refer back to Yin and Yang). The bottom player is protecting the strike zone and the back while trying to get back to a position where they can do jiu jitsu again, and the top player is trying to open the shell to expose the back, the neck, or an arm. Two other positions sit right next to it: turtle is the entry to back control, and it is the position the front headlock is built to punish (the Front Headlock chapter comes next).

Turtle looks like pure defense, and for the bottom player it starts there, with the position built above all to protect the strike zone and the neck. How well the bottom player guards those two, and how patiently the top player works to open them, is most of the exchange.

## The Turtling Player's Job

When I am on the bottom in turtle, I initially shell up to defend my strike zone and neck. My elbows stay in tight, and my hands stay inside and high, up by my neck. The arm is always vulnerable, but keeping elbows tight to the hip and hands near the neck helps keep them locked in place.

Elbows tight make it hard for a foot to get in and become a hook, which is how the top player would start taking the back. Hands up by the neck let me grip fight an incoming choke arm, and, better than that, let me establish grips of my own when that arm comes in. This is inside position in the worst-looking spot on the mat, where the hands and elbows that stay inside still get to decide what happens next. I do deviate from the tight shell, but generally only when I do not feel a direct threat of a hook going in, an attempt being made on my neck, or feel there is space I can take to begin advancing my position.

## The Attacking Player's Job

On top of a turtle, I prefer to stay grounded rather than mobile. I want my weight and connection settled on the opponent instead of circling and hunting from the outside, because a grounded top player keeps the pressure and control that a mobile one keeps giving away.

The top player's decision generally comes down to two paths, taking the back or going to the front headlock, because there is not much you can do directly from the side of a turtle. A few attacks live there, the Peruvian necktie among them, but most of what is available resolves into one of those two directions, the back or the head and neck. The two also trade off against each other, because defending one usually concedes something toward the other, which makes the pair a dilemma rather than a menu.

What I read to choose between them is the bottom player's posture and where they are leaking. If they are tightly covering the hook entry points, elbows and knees closed down to keep a foot from getting in, then the collar grip or the seatbelt is usually free and I work toward the neck and the back by the collar. If their elbows are higher, defending the neck or loading up to shoot a single-leg, then the hook entry point is usually open and I take the back with hooks (the Front Headlock chapter goes through this read in detail). Once I am on the neck I stay on the neck. The one thing that forces me off it is an impending single-leg, which makes me sprawl my legs back and become mobile; from there I either settle back onto the neck, as long as they have not captured my leg, or read that loss of position as the signal to move to the back.

Most of what happens on top of a turtle is still back or front headlock, but a few attacks do live at the side, and they are worth reaching for when the turtle hands them to you. The Peruvian necktie is one. With the crucifix, you trap one of the turtled player's arms with your legs and the other with your arm, which spreads them out and pins both arms at once; it is a pin before any submission, and the same logic of cutting your opponent in half that runs through the rest of the book. The clock choke and its near-side collar cousins attack the neck from the side without having to win the back first.

## Directions Out of the Shell

I usually treat turtle as a temporary position and not a place to stay, and specifically as the place to go when I have ended up faced away from my opponent on the bottom. Turtle lets me protect myself while I turn back in to face them, which is what solves the problem. The same priority shows up throughout the book, because when you are faced away you are exposed, reacting instead of leading, and the first job is always to turn and face the opponent again. The signal to move is often the opponent's own commitment, the choke arm coming in or the weight shifting to take the back, because a committed attacker has given you a direction to turn into.

## Safety

There is not a lot of risk in turtle, honestly, and very little of it is to the spine. The position is structurally sound enough that it is hard to damage the spine without either striking it directly or applying a neck crank. The real risk that does exist is a front headlock driven with heavy downward force, which can become a genuine spine problem. If a neck crank or a choke does come, the response is the same inside position the shell was built on: use your grips to fight the choke arm. The choke arm coming in can also be the signal to move. The room's standard holds here as everywhere: proper chokes, not cranks, and nothing that loads a spine bent forward (you'll find the full standard in the Rules and Safety Note appendix).

## Common Misreads

The bottom player's most common mistake is treating turtle as a home rather than a waypoint. The position exists to protect the strike zone and neck while you turn back in to face the opponent or capture a leg, and sitting in it and waiting only gives the top player time to set up. The other bottom mistakes are mechanical failures of the two jobs: letting the elbows flare, which opens a lane for a hook, or letting the head be controlled and pulled down, which feeds the front headlock. On top, staying mobile and circling gives up the pressure a grounded top game keeps, and hunting for something from the side, where little exists, usually wastes the position instead of committing to the back or the front headlock.

## Training Observation

In turtle exchanges, watch the two protected areas. Are the bottom player's elbows tight and the hands high, or is one of them leaking? A flaring elbow tells you a hook and the back are opening; a loose or lowered head tells you the neck and the front headlock are opening.
