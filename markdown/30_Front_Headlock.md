# Front Headlock

The front headlock is what the top player reaches for when the opponent gives them the head: after a stuffed shot, over a turtled player, or any time posture breaks forward and the head drops below the hips. Control the head and you control a great deal of the body, because the body follows where the head is sent.

It is the natural top response to turtle, and it is a hub for several attacks. From the front headlock the attacker can hunt the guillotine, the anaconda, and the D'Arce, all of which are versions of trapping the head, or the head and an arm together, or abandon the neck to spin to the back. The beauty of these attacks is they share similarities in positions and in grips. Neck attacks and back attacks are the two directions the position resolves into offensively, and they trade off against each other, which makes the pair a dilemma.

## Why the Head Controls the Body

Driving the head down and forward directly manipulates the structure and orientation of the spine. Once the spine is bent and turned out of line, the opponent cannot rebuild a strong posture with the muscles that would normally power an escape. The hips and the core are very strong and the neck is not, so recovering the head means asking the weaker muscles to win against leverage on the spine, caused by the stronger muscles of the top player, and they usually cannot. Weight driven down onto the back of the shoulders makes recovery very difficult because the battle is between the top person's upper body with the benefit of gravity, and the bottom player's shoulders, neck, and head. The head is the handle, and the spine is what it turns.

This is the same lever as spinal alignment, just seen from the top. The clearest way to feel it is from the other side. If someone is passing your guard into side control and you push their head away, you turn their whole body: their head goes toward your feet and their feet come toward your head. Move the head and the body follows, because in both cases you are steering the spine.

## The Attacking Player's Job

The head has to be given up before any of this starts, and most of the time you make the opponent give it. A snap-down does it: threaten up high or feel them reach, then drag the head down and forward so it drops below the hips. A stuffed shot gives it to you for free, because the opponent's own level change drops their head right where you want it, and turtle offers it on a plate. Whichever way you get it, the trigger is the head below the hips, and the first job is just to keep it there while you decide what to hunt.

The front headlock resolves into two directions, the neck or the back, and I choose by reading the bottom player's posture and where they are leaking. Are they tightly covering the hook entry points, elbows and knees closed down to deny a foot going in? Then the collar grip or the seatbelt is likely free, and the neck and the back-by-the-collar are open. Are their elbows higher, defending the neck or loading up for a single-leg? Then the neck is harder but the hook entry point is likely free, and I can take the back with hooks. The leak tells me where to go, the same way heavy and light tells me where to work in side control.

The other thing I read early is whether I have trapped an arm along with the head, because that decides which attacks are even on the table. Head alone, arm out, and I have the guillotine or a clean path to the back. Head and arm together, arm in, and the anaconda, the D'Arce, and the arm-in guillotine open up, because now I have the opponent's own shoulder to drive into the far side of the neck. Trapping the arm makes the strangle heavier, but the same trapped arm hands the defender a specific escape, which is worth knowing before I commit to it.

Once I am on the neck, I stay on the neck. I am not trading it away for something speculative. The one thing that forces me off it is an impending single-leg takedown: when that comes, I have to sprawl my legs back and become mobile, and I cannot hold the same tight position while I do. From there it is a fork of its own. Either I settle back onto the neck, as long as they have not captured my leg, or I read the loss of that settled position as the signal to move to the back. Being forced to sprawl is information, not a defeat: the opponent's committed shot is the thing that hands me the next direction.

## The Defending Player's Job

When people picture defending the front headlock, they picture their head trapped under the opponent's hips. That is not usually where I am. More often I am chest-to-back, or close to it, with the head controlled and a choke arm working across my neck. The situation is defined by the choke, not by the geometry, and that decides what comes first.

Before anything else, read whether your arm is in or out, and if it is in, which strangle you are caught in, because that changes what defending even means.

With the arm out, only the head is trapped, and the neck is the emergency. Go straight to hand-fighting the choke arm. Everything else waits. The neck has no natural defensive system, the hands do that job, and a better position does not save you from a live choke. Secure a clean single-leg while an arm-out guillotine is locked and you are still getting choked. The leg is a real gain and worthless in the same instant, because the arm across your neck did not care that you found a takedown. Kill the choke first, then work back toward posture. That order is where these defenders most often lose it: they feel the scramble open, chase the leg, and hand the finish to an attacker who kept the choke arm in place while they worked.

With the arm in, the defense depends on which strangle you are caught in. The clearest case is the arm-in guillotine, taken when you are on top or square to the attacker. There you have a positional escape: jump to the far side, over the top, so your body ends up across theirs, and your trapped arm and shoulder wedge the neck free as your weight lands on the other side. Unlike the arm-out case, here the position is the choke defense, and it is faster and stronger than grinding a hand fight from underneath.

The anaconda and the D'Arce do not give you that option, because they are usually built from the top, with the attacker over a broken-down opponent. You cannot jump across when you are the one being flattened. Against those two you have to start defending earlier: deny the arm-and-neck connection before it locks, keep the trapped elbow and the knee line alive so the attacker cannot isolate you and roll, and above all do not let the head be driven down past the hips in the first place. Once the structure is fully built, these two strangles are beaten more by recognizing the danger early than by any single escape.

Once the choke is dealt with, whichever version you are in, you are trying to get the head back over the hips, because as long as the head is below the hips the spine stays bent and the attacker keeps the lever. You can get there by hand-fighting free and posturing up, by jumping across against an arm-in guillotine, or by standing and running the pipe through to the back.

## The Strangles as a Family

The guillotine, the anaconda, and the D'Arce look like three different submissions, and at the moment of the finish they are. Underneath, they are one family, and reading them comes down to three variables: where the strangling arm sits, where the grip locks, and the orientation of the body.

Start with what all three are doing to the neck, because that is what makes the variables mean something. A strangle closes the two carotid arteries on the sides of the neck, and these attacks close both at once, one directly and one indirectly. Obviously, a guillotine that is not arm-in is different, but for the purposes of this discussion, assume an arm-in guillotine.

Direct pressure is a part of your own arm pressed against the carotid artery. Indirect pressure is the opponent's own deltoid driven into the carotid artery on the other side. The anaconda and the D'Arce both do exactly this. What changes between them is which part of your strangling arm meets the carotid artery, and which part sits outside the deltoid to bury it into the opposite carotid artery.

In the anaconda, your bicep presses into the near carotid artery for the direct pressure, and your grip locks outside the trapped arm, so your hand and lower arm sit outside the deltoid and drive it into the far carotid artery. The finish tends to come from displacing the post elbow with your inside knee and rolling through, and finishing mechanics involve capturing the opponent's top-side arm to aid in adding pressure.

The D'Arce threads the same arm one segment deeper. Now the direct pressure comes from the wrist and forearm laid along the near carotid artery, while the bicep sits outside the deltoid and buries it into the far carotid artery, with the grip locked on the neck side rather than outside the arm. The two carotid arteries and the direct-and-indirect pattern stay the same; the strangling arm has just slid through so a different part of it does each job.

The guillotine is the anaconda's hand and grip run from a different body position. The structure at the neck is the same; you either sit back into a guard-like position and extend, or stay tall and pull up into standing with pressure driven onto the back.

So the read is three questions. Which part of your arm meets the carotid artery, the bicep or the wrist and forearm? Where is the grip, outside the trapped arm or on the neck side? And what is the body doing, rolling, sitting, or standing? Answer those and you know which strangle you have, the same way a passer who reads his position knows which pass is available. All three still obey the book's oldest rule about finishing: the front headlock is the pin, and the strangle is what the pin was for.

## Safety

The room rule on cranks holds here, and it is more precise than just "no cranks." A crank is acceptable as long as it is not forcefully applied. Cranks are reasonable and useful to train if applied with care rather than with force rapidly applied. Applied with measure, a crank is often a legitimate setup to a choke rather than an end in itself. From front turtle on top, I can set the guillotine position and crank the neck to the side, then use the free hand to push the face back into the choking arm. That is usually what locks the choking arm onto the neck, the jaw popping over the forearm, and the finish is a proper blood choke. The crank was a mechanism, not the goal.

So the line is force and speed, not the presence of a crank. A measured crank that turns the head to complete a choke is within the standard. A neck wrenched or jerked as the finish itself is not, and it is the fastest way to hurt a partner who has jobs, a family, and a life off the mat (you'll find the full standard in the Rules and Safety Note appendix). Apply slowly, respect the tap, and let the crank become a choke instead of a punishment.

## Common Misreads

The bottom player's most common mistake is applying the wrong defense because they never read whether the arm is in or out. With the arm out, that looks like chasing a positional escape while the choke stays live: a single-leg feels like progress, but an arm-out guillotine locked across the neck finishes anyway. Against an arm-in guillotine, it is the opposite error: grinding a hand fight from underneath when jumping to the far side, over the top, would have lifted the neck free faster. Sitting under all of it is the same feeding mistake: letting the head be driven down and forward until the grip they fear becomes easy to keep.

The top player's mistakes mirror the turtle chapter. Treating the crank as the finish instead of a measured setup to the choke turns a controlled attack into a wrench that risks a partner and often still does not finish. And trading a settled neck for a speculative back take, instead of staying on the neck until an impending single-leg forces the sprawl, gives away the control the position was built on.

## Training Observation

In front-headlock exchanges, watch two things. First, read the strangle through its three variables: which part of the strangling arm meets the carotid artery (the bicep or the wrist and forearm), where the grip is locked (outside the trapped arm, or on the neck side), and what the body is doing (rolling through, sitting to guard, or standing). Remember that the anaconda and the D'Arce both close one carotid artery directly and drive the deltoid into the other. Bicep on the carotid artery with the grip outside the arm is the anaconda, or the guillotine when it finishes from guard or standing; forearm on the carotid artery with a neck-side grip is the D'Arce. Second, note whether the defender's arm is in or out, then watch whether the defense fits. Arm out, the hand fight on the choke arm is the answer, and reaching for a leg while the neck stays trapped is about to teach that a takedown does not beat a choke. Against an arm-in guillotine, the answer is positional, jumping to the far side over the top, and grinding a hand fight from underneath is the slow road; the anaconda and D'Arce, taken from the top, are beaten earlier, before the connection locks.
