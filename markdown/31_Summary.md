# Summary

Jiu jitsu will always be bigger than any book, instructor, academy, or system. That is part of what makes it worth studying. No matter how long you train, there will be another detail to understand, another problem to solve, another body type to deal with, another rule set to adjust to, and another student who asks a question that makes you rethink something you thought you already knew.

This book was never designed to give you every answer or to cover every position, every detail, every offensive motion, every defensive maneuver, every sweep, every counter. Its purpose has been to give you a clearer way to mentally model the big picture behind the majority of positions in which you will find yourself. I hope you have found value in the material.

When you are stuck, ask what battle you are losing. Look at head height, hip height, grips, and posture and base, then at the quadrant you are trying to move through and the post that is defending it. Run the Konami Code and ask which directions are real: up, down, left, and right; backward, forward, and diagonal. Look at centerline, and ask whether your elbow has crossed the sternum and whether your opponent has a path to your back. Look at inside position and the strike zone, and ask whether your elbow, knee, head, or spine is still helping you or has become a handle. Look for two-on-one control, and if one limb has been made unreliable, work out what that changes. Look for what has been isolated and what needs to be reconnected. Find the fulcrum, the lever, the trapped post, or the missing angle. Notice whether the spine is aligned or whether the body has been divided into pieces that no longer work together. Ask what your opponent wants, name the opposite job, and look for the dilemma being created.

When you are winning, ask whether you are pressing the advantage or sitting on it. A position is a means, not a place to live, and control is worth holding only when it enables advancement toward the next pin, isolation, or threat. Ask which battle you have already won, what enabled it, and whether you are spending time wisely on it or wasting it. Have you secured the pin before reaching for the finish, or are you grabbing at a submission the control cannot yet support? Every submission starts as a pin, so make the pin real first. Ask whether you are cutting your opponent in half or only holding the pieces you have. When you make space, take it, because making without taking hands the space right back to them. Watch the kind of connection you feel. When a control starts to feel like hanging on with the arms, it is already leaving, and the time to convert is while the hips are still yours. To set a dilemma, give the opponent a threat they have to answer, then establish position in a way that forces their response to open the next one. Do not relax when you are winning. Keep doing jiu jitsu while the other person is stuck choosing the lesser of two evils.

Those questions do not replace techniques. Learn techniques methodically, step-by-step, and pay attention to the details, because they matter. The concepts presented here make techniques easier to understand. They also make failure more useful telemetry for how to improve, because a specific failure can be trained and a vague failure only becomes frustration. Instead of leaving a round with only "my guard got passed," you can leave with "my hips were pinned before I addressed the grips," or "I lost the underhook and accepted being flat," or "I chased the choke before I separated the hands."

## The Questions to Keep

Carry these questions into training:

- What battle is deciding the exchange?
- Whose head height, hip height, grips, and posture and base are better organized?
- Which quadrant am I moving through?
- Which Konami Code directions are real from here?
- Whose centerline is protected, and whose limbs have become handles?
- Who has inside position?
- Who controls the strike zone from armpit to hip?
- Has a two-on-one made one limb unreliable?
- What part of the body has been isolated?
- What needs to be reconnected?
- Where is the fulcrum, and what is the lever?
- Which post will stop the sweep?
- Whose spine is aligned?
- What does each person want from this position?
- What is the opposite job?
- What response am I forcing, and what does that response expose?

The list is not meant to be recited in order during a round. Under pressure, having one good situationally-relevant question in your back pocket is enough. If you are under side control, that question may be "Can I turn and reconnect my knee and elbow?" If you are attacking mount, it may be "Can I separate an elbow without losing base?" In closed guard, either "Which quadrant is still defended?" or "Which posture response am I forcing?" may organize the exchange. And in a leg entanglement, "Is my knee line free?" may be the only question that matters for the next two seconds.

After you finish the book, do not walk into the next round trying to see all of it at once. Choose one or two items that caught your attention and search for one of them. Highlight the pages you want to test, write in the margins, or keep a journal if that fits you better. You may not find the thing you were looking for, and you may notice an earlier idea that did not feel significant the first time you read it. That is how the book should work, because its job is to point you back to the mat, then let the mat tell you what needs your attention next.

## Principles Need Rounds

A concept that sounds clean on the page will get tested by blood, sweat, and tears, by timing, fatigue, fear, and resistance, and by the stubborn intelligence of another person trying to beat you. That test is what makes you better: having another human being with a similar goal test you and attempt to impose their will against yours situationally. Relish it, be thankful for it, appreciate it for what it is.

Do not protect the ideas in this book from the mat. Bring them to training and let them become more precise. If a principle fails, ask why. Was the idea wrong, or was the timing wrong? Was there an exception? Did the rule set change the incentive? Did the opponent solve a different battle first? Did you understand the words but not the feel?

Or was the guidance I gave you incorrect? Like you, I'm human, prone to mistakes, and also like you, on the journey of improving my own jiu jitsu game. I invite you to give me candid feedback on what works, what doesn't, what key concepts I have missed, and how my communication of those concepts could be improved. One of the most beautiful aspects of this art is that it first teaches humility, and as a black belt, I have been served more than enough beatdowns to provide me the humility necessary to accept that this work is incomplete and can be refined.

Good jiu jitsu is not blind loyalty to techniques or systems. It is the repeated refinement of reliable principles under changing conditions.

## The Day I Got My Black Belt

I knew the day was coming because Ron told me. I was driving almost three hours to attend the ceremony, so he had to let me know. Even with that warning, the day still felt enormous. It felt like validation of the time, the effort, the years of being tired, sore, confused, humbled, and dragged back into the room by the knowledge that I was getting better even when I could not always see it.

I was the first belt presented that day, though one of my training partners and someone for whom I hold deep respect, Emilio Perez, would have been first before me if he had not been late for good reason. Ron had me remove my brown belt. He tied the black belt around my waist. The room applauded, and it was full of people I had trained with for years, along with people I had never trained with at all. I hadn't been a regular in the academy for over a year, and there were a lot of new faces mixed in with familiar ones. I was riding high. It felt like standing at the top of a very long climb in one of the hardest martial arts in the world, receiving one of the hardest belts to earn.

Then Ron said, "now give a speech."

I was not prepared.

Public speaking has been part of my professional life in technology for a long time, sometimes in demanding rooms with large audiences spanning those with deep technical expertise to those with three commas in their net worth, so I composed myself. I talked about why I started jiu jitsu, what it had come to mean to me, what the black belt represented, and how much the AKA family and the larger jiu jitsu community meant to me. Then I discussed three ideas.

Details matter. Be open to learning, relearning, succeeding, and failing. Find the why behind everything. Ask why something works, why it does not work, and why something that normally works failed that one time.

Two things never lie: the mats, and iron. They expose what you know and what you do not know. They expose fear, anxiety, and insecurity; confidence and false confidence; and how you see yourself and how you see other people. A hard round can reveal truths that ordinary conversation will never reach. And iron is solid: mass, weight, and either you can move it or you can't. But what's more important is if you are progressing toward being able to move it.

Just keep showing up. Stripes, belts, and progression all matter, but the work is the journey itself, which means personal growth, learning how to operate your body, learning how to think under pressure, and learning how to continue when the room keeps telling you the truth.

Shakira's "Try Everything" catches part of that same idea in four words: "I keep falling down." In jiu jitsu you get passed, tapped, swept, flattened, and confused. Then you start again, only with better information, and the habit to build is getting up with enough honesty to ask what the fall exposed.

The black belt validated the years, but it only started the work. In some ways, it made it more clear: fundamentals are not behind you when you reach black belt. They become the focal lens through which you keep studying.

If you close this book with more questions, that is not a failure, because more questions, and better and more granular ones, are the beginning of more intentional, focused training. The goal is to see more of what was already there and confidently navigate in uncertainty, not to make jiu jitsu smaller.

With much love for you, the reader, Joel
