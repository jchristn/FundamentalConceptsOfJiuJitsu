# Glossary

Jiu jitsu words can sound familiar and still mean different things from room to room. A short glossary will not settle every argument, but it gives this book a shared vocabulary. When a chapter says posture, base, frame, quadrant, Konami Code, or something else, the word should point to something concrete enough to observe during training.

## Alignment

Alignment is the useful relationship between the head, spine, hips, limbs, and base. It does not mean being straight or stiff; the structure changes by position.

## Base

Base is the ability to resist unwanted movement. Depending on the exchange, the hands, feet, knees, elbows, hips, head position, and grips can all act as base.

Good base meets the threat in front of you; a base that prevents one problem may create another.

## Centerline

The centerline is the invisible line running through the middle of the body. Elbows, knees, head position, and spinal direction all relate to it. When an elbow reaches or passes the sternum under opponent control, the opponent has a path to the back.

Crossing the centerline is not forbidden, and advanced movement often does it deliberately. Ask who owns the exposure: whether you chose it with timing and an exit, or the opponent forced it and kept control.

## Center of Gravity

Center of gravity is the point where a player's weight can be loaded, moved, or made difficult to move.

## Connection

Connection is meaningful contact that changes movement, posture, balance, or choices. A grip, hook, frame, chest connection, head position, or hip clamp can all create connection.

Contact is connection only when it makes the opponent solve a problem or creates an opportunity for you.

## Cooking

Cooking is controlling and applying pressure long enough to drain the opponent mentally and physically before advancing. Unlike empty stalling, it uses pressure, pinning, and discomfort to make the opponent's next responses weaker, later, or more predictable.

## Deep Half

Deep half is a bottom half guard variation where the bottom player gets underneath the top player's hips and controls the leg relationship from below.

## Dilemma

A dilemma is a pair of threats that make the opponent's correct responses conflict with each other. Defending one threat exposes the other. Two techniques form a dilemma only when they have a real mechanical relationship.

## Escalation

Escalation is the decision to answer a lost position, submission threat, or hard round by increasing danger beyond what the training relationship can support. It is not the same as intensity. Hard training can be honest and controlled. Escalation turns learning into injury risk.

## Facing the Opponent

Facing the opponent is generally ensuring you are looking in the direction of your opponent and oriented toward them.

## Frame

A frame is structure placed between your body and the opponent's pressure. Frames create space, preserve alignment, protect centerline, and make movement possible. A frame controls distance by transferring pressure through structure rather than muscle.

Frames and levers can use the same body parts. The job separates them. A frame is generally defensive and helps recover ground. A lever is generally offensive and helps move, expose, or isolate something.

## Fulcrum

The fulcrum is the point around which force becomes meaningful, the point over which movement occurs.

Because leverage is sensitive to placement, a small change in the fulcrum can change the whole position.

## Grip

A grip is a connection to your opponent through your limbs, typically your hands. Grips are useful only when they control movement, posture, alignment, base, reaction, or access. Gi grips may use collars, sleeves, pants, lapels, and belts. No-gi grips may use wrists, elbows, underhooks, and overhooks, along with head position, body locks, clamps, and hand fighting.

Holding on to the opponent does not control them unless the grip prevents something or makes something possible.

## Heel Hook

The heel hook is a rotational leg attack through the heel and against the knee, where control of the knee line and hip determines both danger and defense. Because feedback can arrive late, heel-hook training should emphasize control, clear mechanics, early recognition, and catch and release.

## Hip Line

The hip line is the relationship between the opponent's hips and your ability to control, pass, sweep, enter, or escape. Many positions are decided by whether the hips are mobile, pinned, elevated, turned, blocked, or connected to the floor.

## Hip-Side Frame

The hip-side frame is a bottom side control frame connecting the elbow to the hip rather than laying the forearm across it. The elbow-to-hip structure carries weight through the skeleton; a forearm on the hip collapses under a small amount of downward pressure.

## Inside Position

Inside position means your frames, limbs, head, or grips occupy the useful space closer to the center of your body than the opponent's control.

Inside position lets your body work in front of the problem instead of reaching around it from a weak angle.

## Isolation

Isolation is the process of separating a vulnerable part of your opponent from the rest of their body. An arm separated from the ribs, a neck separated from hand defense, a knee line separated from hip freedom, or a shoulder separated from spinal alignment can all become targets.

Submissions become more efficient and effective after isolation. Escapes usually begin by reconnecting what has been isolated.

## Knee-Elbow Connection

Knee-elbow connection is the defensive relationship between the elbow and knee on a side of the body. It protects the space the opponent wants to enter. When the knee and elbow separate, the opponent can climb, pass, isolate, or pin. When they reconnect, the defender regains a layer.

## Knee Line

The knee line is the position of the knee relative to the opponent's controlling legs, hips, or grips in leg entanglements. If the knee line is trapped, the defender may still be vulnerable even if the foot feels partly free. If the knee line is clear, many leg attacks lose power. Knee-line awareness is often a safety priority as much as an escape priority because some leg locks involve rotational force and late feedback.

## Knee Shield

The knee shield is a half guard structure involving the bottom player's top leg, used to keep distance from the opponent. It manages pressure and can conceal the underhook-side arm beneath the knee.

## Konami Code

The Konami Code is a directional diagnostic borrowed from old video games. In jiu jitsu, it asks what options exist up, down, left, and right from a position, as well as backward, forward, and diagonally.

The Konami Code is an opportunity scan, not a promise that every direction is available. Sometimes a blocked direction is useful because it tells the player where to look next or what reaction to create.

## Lever

The lever is the structure used to apply movement, pressure, rotation, or bending. A lever becomes stronger when it is long, aligned, and connected to larger parts of the body.

When a body part is only blocking pressure so the defender can recover, it is usually a frame. When that same structure begins turning, lifting, redirecting, or exposing the opponent, it has become a lever.

## Lockdown

Lockdown is a half guard leg configuration where the bottom player brings the outside leg's foot inside and creates a double-foot connection underneath the shin of the top player's entangled leg. Lockdown allows the bottom player to control and extend the opponent's trapped leg. It can pull the opponent in, push the opponent away, drive them backward, or otherwise maneuver their body.

## Log Roll

A log roll is a sweep from underneath where the bottom player loads the top player's weight and center of gravity onto their own body while eliminating a post and rolls the opponent in the direction of the eliminated post. It is most available when the top player's center of gravity is directly over the bottom player's, or when the bottom player can scoot underneath and create that alignment.

## Making and Taking Space

Making space means creating an opening through frames, pressure, movement, angle, posture breaking, or opponent reaction. Taking space means claiming that opening with a grip, knee, elbow, or hook, or with a hip angle, head position, or connection, before the opponent reclaims that space.

## Mount Height Dilemma

The mount height dilemma is the trade the top player makes by choosing how high to ride the bottom player. Low mount, over the hips, owns the legs but leaves the bridging, trap-and-roll, and elbow-knee escapes available. High mount, up the chest, defeats those escapes but concedes the legs back to the bottom player and opens the back door as an escape path as the top player climbs.

## Obstacle

An obstacle is anything the opponent must clear, staple, redirect, pin, or make useless before they can advance. Feet, knees, shins, hooks, frames, grips, hip angle, and head position can all become obstacles.

In this book, passing guard means getting obstacles out of the way, and playing guard from bottom means putting them in the way. Good obstacles create push and pull levers, protect space, and force the opponent to solve a problem.

## Pin 1, Pin 2, and Pin 3

Pin 1, Pin 2, and Pin 3 are Rudy Sanchez's side-control pin framework as used in this book.

Pin 1 has the head-side arm on the near side. It is strong for control, particularly with chest pressure and spinal misalignment, but offers less submission access because the top player's hands are split on both sides of the body.

Pin 2 has the head-side arm on the far side but above the far shoulder. It can give strong control and better attack access because both hands are on the same side, opening far-side arm attacks, technical mount, S-mount, and related progressions.

Pin 3 has the head-side arm on the far side of the body covering the strike zone. It can create strong oblique pressure into the chest and belly, but the top player is facing away from the opponent, so grips behind the back, hips, waistline, knees, or legs become the priority.

## Point System

The basic point system used in this book follows the IBJJF ruleset, which provides two points for takedowns, sweeps, and knee on belly; three points for passing the guard; and four points for mount, back mount, or back control with hooks. Points are not the purpose of the book, but they show which forms of control the sport tends to reward.

## Post

A post is anything that catches balance or stops movement. A hand, foot, knee, or elbow can become a post, and so can the head, a hip, or a grip. Sweeps often work because the post that would stop the fall has been removed, trapped, redirected, or overloaded.

## Posture

Posture is useful structure under pressure. A straight back or upright head is not enough by itself. A player with posture can move, defend, apply force, and make decisions without collapsing. Posture changes by position but always depends on alignment, base, and the ability to keep the body organized while pressure changes.

## Quadrant

Quadrants divide the opponent's base and weight into eight useful directions: up, down, left, and right, plus the four diagonals of upper left, upper right, lower left, and lower right. A quadrant is defended when a post (a hand, knee, foot, hip, head, or grip) blocks movement through it.

## Rodeo Into S-Mount

Rodeo into S-mount is a possible transition out of side control or side-back style control when the top player rides the defender's turn and arrives in an S-mount attacking shape. This book mentions it only as a reference point and does not cover it in detail.

## Shrimp

A shrimp, also called a hip escape, is a movement where the bottom player uses the hands or elbows as frames, turns to the side facing the opponent, and moves the hips away from the opponent, making space that they can then take using their legs.

## Side-Back Control

Side-back control is a transitional control where the top player is beginning to own the defender's back exposure before full back control is established.

## Smash Passing

Smash passing is a pressure passing family that keeps connection to the bottom player while systematically advancing toward the pass.

## Straight Ankle Lock

The straight ankle lock is a leg attack where the attacker controls the leg and applies force typically through the area around the Achilles tendon. For this book, it is the basic leg-lock example for studying posture, leg control, knee-line awareness, hip mobility, and safe finishing mechanics.

## Strike Zone

The strike zone is the area from armpit to hip. If the opponent controls that space, they can exert control over where you can and cannot go, deny frames, pin hips, expose back paths, and open advancement.

## Two-on-One

A two-on-one is a control relationship where two of your limbs control one opponent limb. The control can direct where the opponent's body is aimed, how they can move, and what their free limbs can do. The simplest version is two hands controlling one arm, but the concept can also include sleeve-and-tricep control, wrist-and-elbow control, a trapped whizzer arm, or emergency control over the choking arm from back defense.

## Vertical Shoulders

Vertical shoulders describe bottom half guard posture where the bottom player is lying on a side with one shoulder oriented above the other instead of flat on the back. Vertical shoulders make the position mobile. Flat shoulders usually mean the top player is beginning to smash, crossface, and pass.

## Wedge

A wedge blocks movement by occupying space the opponent needs. It may not submit or sweep by itself, but it makes the next action possible by denying the opponent's path. A frame holds space and a wedge blocks a path, and the same body part can do both depending on the angle.

## Wrist Lock

A wrist lock is a legitimate submission, not a cheap trick, but the room standard still applies. Go for it. Do not rip it. Respect the tap, respect the partner, and remember that training partners have jobs, lives, and activities outside jiu jitsu.
