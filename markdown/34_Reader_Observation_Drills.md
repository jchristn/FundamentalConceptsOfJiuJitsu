# Reader Observation Drills

The drills in this appendix give you a way to watch and train the concepts without turning the book into a move catalog, and they do not replace technical instruction. Do each drill lightly enough that both partners can observe the position and honestly enough that the answers matter.

Use short rounds. Two or three minutes is enough. Pick one question and stay with it, because the goal is to make one part of the exchange easier to see, not to win the room that day.

## Head Height, Hip Height, Grips, and Posture and Base

Start in any live round and pick one failed exchange. Do not name the technique first. Name the first battle that changed: head height, hip height, grips, or posture and base.

For a more focused version, begin in a common position such as closed guard, half guard, or side control. One player tries to improve position. The other player resists normally. After each reset, both partners identify which battle decided the exchange.

## Quadrants

Start in closed guard with a scissor-sweep style setup. The bottom player controls one sleeve and collar. The top player defends by posting with the hand, widening the knee, shifting the hips, or changing posture.

The bottom player may not force the same direction twice. They must name the defended quadrant, change the loading, and move the top player toward a different quadrant. The top player tracks which post saved them and what that post exposed.

## Konami Code

Start in bottom half guard with vertical shoulders. The bottom player names a direction before moving: up, down, left, or right; backward, forward, or diagonal. They may not repeat the same direction twice in a row.

The top player gives normal resistance and names what they defended. After the round, both players decide which directions were real and which were fake. A real direction has posture, grips, inside position, and connection behind it.

## Centerline

Start from side control, mount, or seated open guard. The only observation is elbow position. One partner tries to pull or force an elbow across centerline. The other tries to keep the elbow useful without becoming stiff.

After the round, ask what changed when the elbow reached or crossed the sternum. Did the shoulder turn and the spine follow? Did back exposure appear, or did a frame become weak?

## Inside Position

Start from side control, open guard, back control, or a wrestling tie. One player tries to win the inside space: inside frame, inside knee, inside hand, underhook, or head position. The other player tries to recover inside position before advancing or escaping.

After each reset, ask which structure was inside and whether it actually had a job. A hand inside with the elbow flared may not be useful, while an elbow, knee, or frame that protects the line of pressure usually is.

## Strike Zone

Start from side control, mount, or seated open guard. Name the defender's strike zone: armpit to hip. The attacker tries to control that lane without rushing the finish. The defender tries to keep at least one elbow, knee, frame, or hip angle useful inside it.

After the round, ask what strike-zone control allowed: did it flatten the defender, expose the back, deny a frame, open a pass, or begin a submission process?

## Two-on-One

Start from closed guard, half guard, bottom side control, bottom mount, or back defense. One player tries to put two useful controls on one limb. The other player tries to recover that limb without giving away posture, base, centerline, or the next positional step.

From closed guard, use a pistol grip at the end of whichever sleeve is in front of you and a tricep or C-grip behind the elbow on the same arm. The bottom player turns the hips and body, extends both arms, and studies whether the top player's back begins to appear or whether the near-side post disappears when the top player drives back in.

From half guard, let the top player whizzer the bottom player's underhook. The bottom player uses the bottom hand to control the end of the sleeve on the whizzer arm. Pause there and ask whether the top player can still post that hand to stop the sweep.

Name what the controlled limb can no longer do: whether a post disappeared, the body turned, the whizzer stopped being a solution and became a handle, or the choking arm became less dangerous. Also check whether straight arms made the controlled limb hard to recover. If the limb can still do its main job without consequence, the control is only a grip.

## Isolation and Reconnection

Start from mount, back control, or closed guard. The attacker wins by isolating one arm, one defensive hand, or the neck without rushing a finish. The defender wins by reconnecting the isolated part to the body.

Keep the finish out of the first round so the attacker can learn whether the isolation is real and the defender can learn what reconnection feels like before the submission itself is the problem.

## Fulcrums, Levers, and Posts

Pick one sweep both partners know. The sweeping player may enter the sweep only after naming the post that will stop it. The defending player tries to recover or replace that post.

After a few rounds, switch the focus. The defender names the post they need back before the sweep happens. The attacker tries to remove, trap, redirect, or overload it first.

## Making and Taking Space

Pick any recovery or any pass and split it into two beats out loud. One player names the moment space was made, whether by a frame, a bridge, a push, or a broken posture, and the other names the moment it was taken with a knee inserted, an underhook won, a grip landed, or a hip slid to a new angle. Check nothing except the gap between the two beats.

Then add a rule: the player who makes space must claim it before the next breath, or the round resets. If they make space and do not take it, the other player takes it back, and both name how. Making space is not the escape or the advance; taking it is.

## Spinal Alignment

Start from side control bottom. The top player controls lightly enough that the bottom player can move but honestly enough that pressure exists. The bottom player may not bridge hard until they first recover head direction, inside frame, or hip angle.

Add a second round where the top player turns the bottom player's face away with controlled head or collar pressure. The bottom player compares the strength of the hip thrust before and after the spine is misaligned.

Add a third round where the top player compares straight downward shoulder pressure with shoulder pressure across the jaw and down. The bottom player does not need to escape, only to report which version changes head direction, hip power, and the ability to build an inside frame.

Feel the difference between movement from alignment and movement from panic. A strong bridge from bad alignment often wastes energy, while a smaller movement from better alignment often opens the escape.

## Yin and Yang

Choose one position and have both partners state their job before the round starts. Keep the statements short.

Examples:

- Side control top: keep them flat, deny inside frames, separate elbows.
- Side control bottom: turn, frame inside, reconnect knee and elbow.
- Mount top: separate elbows and climb.
- Bottom mount: protect neck, reconnect elbows, create angle.
- Back attacker: separate hands and keep shoulder control.
- Back defender: protect neck, win hands, move shoulders toward alignment.

After the round, ask which job happened first.

## Dilemmas

Start in closed guard. The bottom player tries to create a posture dilemma. If the top player stays low or broken, the bottom player works toward a sweep or posture-control attack. If the top player postures up, the bottom player looks for kimura, guillotine, hip bump, or a similar sit-up threat.

The top player wins by recovering posture without giving away exposed elbows, neck position, or predictable posts. After the round, both players name the response that was forced and what that response exposed.

## Open Guard

Start in open guard with normal resistance. The guard player may not attack a sweep or submission until they have created either angle or off-balance. The passer may not settle chest-to-chest until they have addressed the hips.

The round exposes common fake progress. The guard player may have grips but no angle, and the passer may clear feet but never control the hips.

Add an obstacle round. The bottom player names what they are putting in the way: knee shield, shin, frame, or hook; sleeve grip, collar grip, hip angle, or head position. The passer names what they are getting out of the way and how: clearing, stapling, redirecting, pinning, or making it useless. If either player cannot name the obstacle, they pause and reset.

Add a frame-versus-lever round. The bottom player identifies one structure that begins as a defensive frame. If that same structure later turns, lifts, redirects, or exposes the passer, both players name when it stopped being only a frame and started acting as a lever.

For a half-guard version, start with knee shield and collar-sleeve. The bottom player uses the collar-grip side to help preserve distance first, then shoots the knee through and lets the arm become the underhook. The checkpoint is whether the same side of the body changed jobs from keeping space to taking space.

## Closed Guard

Start in closed guard. Bottom player wins by breaking posture and creating angle. Top player wins by rebuilding posture and opening the guard without exposing elbows across centerline.

Do a second round where submissions are allowed only after posture is broken, so the bottom player has to earn the attack instead of jumping to the final position.

## Half Guard

Start in half guard and name the position by battles instead of label: flat or on side, shoulders vertical or shoulders pinned, underhook or crossface, knee trapped or knee free, hips mobile or pinned.

Half guard is not one position, so reset whenever one player clearly changes two of those battles.

For a more direct version, start with the bottom player on vertical shoulders. Top wins the reset by controlling the head and putting the shoulders on the mat. Bottom wins the reset by keeping vertical shoulders long enough to choose a real path: knee shield for distance, underhook for connection, far-side arm two-on-one for the kimura/underhook dilemma, or a return from deep half back to vertical shoulders when the top player drops weight and moves the hips away.

Then isolate the transition. Start in knee shield. Bottom removes the shield and tries to get the underhook, leg control, or wrestle-up connection. Top tries to win the crossface or head control before that connection settles. The round ends as soon as either player clearly wins the race.

For making and taking space, start with bottom half guard, knee shield, and collar-sleeve grips, with the collar-grip arm hidden under the knee shield. Bottom shoots the knee through and lets the arm travel with it, releases the sleeve grip, and comes up on that hand. The first checkpoint is whether the leg and arm made space. The second checkpoint is whether the bottom player took that space by getting the underhook and pinning the head to the top player's chest, abdomen, or hip.

For deep half, start with the bottom player underneath and both partners name whose center of gravity is over whose. Bottom wins by aligning the centers enough to log roll or by using lockdown extension and leg control to pull, push, extend, or rebuild vertical shoulders. Top wins by scooting the hips and base away without leaving space the bottom player can take.

For top half, let the top player choose between a standing pass and smash passing. After each reset, ask what changed when the top player stood or stayed connected. Did standing create passing freedom, or did it give the bottom player a chance to stand or wrestle? Did smash passing remove choices, or did the bottom player recover space?

Add a smash-passing pressure round. Top starts with head and shoulder control and works to free the entangled leg inch by inch using the outside instep. Bottom wins by recovering a side angle, head height, or enough obstacles to slow the pass. Top loses the round if the weight shifts too high over the bottom player's head and pressure disappears.

## Side Control

Start from side control. Bottom player tracks three questions: can I turn my head, can I turn my hips, can I reconnect knee and elbow? Top player tracks the same questions from the opposite side: which one am I denying?

Do not require a full escape or submission. The round ends when one player clearly wins or loses the structural battle.

Add a pin round. The top player chooses Pin 1, Pin 2, or Pin 3 and names it before the round begins. The bottom player names the likely recovery lane before moving. After the reset, both players identify where the top player was heavy, where the top player was light, and whether the hip-side hand was doing a job.

For early students, begin the pin round with Pin 1. The top player studies the instinctual pattern: head control, body control, and pressure down. The bottom player studies which frames can still get inside that pressure.

In a utility-hand round, the top player may not attack a submission until the hip-side hand has done something useful: block half-guard recovery, catch an underhook attempt, push or pull a knee, cover the hips, cover the waistline, or post to stop movement.

Add an orientation round. The top player changes between chest-to-chest, hip-to-chest, perpendicular, angled toward the head, angled toward the feet, and off-center pressure. Without needing to escape, the bottom player names which area became lighter and which recovery path became more realistic.

For a cooking round, start from Pin 1. The top player does not advance until the tempo changes. The bottom player stops being explosive, settles into the position, and starts answering later. After the reset, both players name what changed: breathing, frames, knee movement, posture, or panic.

Add a Pin 1 placeholder round. The top player may hold Pin 1 long enough to reset the tempo, but then must advance to Pin 2, knee on belly, mount, side-back control, or rodeo into S-mount. The bottom player wins by using the reset to rebuild enough structure to start doing jiu jitsu again.

In a Pin 2 far-side arm round, the top player does not need to finish, only to show how hip-on-chest pressure brings the far-side arm closer to attack. Compare the straight arm lock and kimura by control point: deltoid or top of tricep for the straight arm lock, elbow line for the kimura grip. The bottom player studies whether the urge to withdraw the arm makes them turn away from the top player, create distance toward the trapped arm, and expose the back. The top player wins the reaction by shifting weight toward the top arm, preventing the bottom player from turning back in, and taking side-back control.

Add a Pin 3 attachment round. The bottom player may push the back or latch to belt or pants to protect the far-side arm. The top player studies whether that attachment creates a hole. Thread outside for the quick wristlock. Thread inside and drive forward to retain control, add downward force, and return toward Pin 2 attacks.

Add a bottom recovery round. The bottom player starts by building frames, pushing away to make space, pivoting the body away, and inserting the bottom knee. The hip-side frame must be elbow-to-hip, not forearm-to-hip. If frames are difficult, they identify whether the top player is burying the head, shelving the inside arm, or making the frame collapse through downward pressure. If the arm is shelved, the bottom player wins the reset by making enough distance to retract the arm and rebuild structure. The goal is to create one inch of space and take it before the top player takes it back.

Add a hip-side frame test before the round. First, put the bottom player's forearm across the waist with the hand on the hip and have the top player apply controlled weight. Then put the bottom player's elbow on the hip and aim the elbow straight up. Both partners should feel how much easier it is for the elbow-to-hip frame to carry weight. After that, restart the recovery round and require the bottom player to take any space they make by building vertical shoulders, inserting a knee, rebuilding a frame, or pushing the top player away.

For a side-back exposure round, the bottom player begins from the mistake of having turned away under side control. Their only goal is to turn and face the opponent before back control becomes fully established. They should protect vertical shoulders during the turn and avoid being flattened again. The top player wins by staying behind the defender's ability to face them.

## Knee on Belly

Start from knee on belly. The top player tries to create predictable reactions without losing base. The bottom player tries to recover structure without panic pushing.

The bottom player also studies whether pulling the top player's ankle toward the face turns the top player's knee and creates movement. The first bottom response matters most. Did it protect centerline, misalign the top player's base, or expose an arm, neck, back, or post?

Add a grips-read round. The bottom player calls the top player's intent out loud from the hands alone: a pull on the lapels reads as pinning and cooking, a push or neutral pressure reads as readiness to move. Then the top player tries to fool the read by pulling as if to pin and moving anyway, because grips signal intent and a good top player can bait the reaction.

Add a step-to-mount round. The top player may only try to advance from knee on belly to mount. The bottom player wins by catching the leg on the step before mount lands, which means seeing the transition as the moment of opportunity as well as a threat.

## Mount

Start in low mount. The top player wins by separating one elbow and climbing without losing base. The bottom player wins by reconnecting one elbow and creating a useful angle without exposing the neck.

In a second round, begin in high mount. The bottom player works only on elbow recovery and neck protection. The top player works only on maintaining the climb and forcing a defensive reaction.

Add an obstacle round from high mount. The top player chooses one objective: expose the neck or expose an elbow. The bottom player tries to put hands, elbows, frames, or angles back in the way. The top player wins by clearing the obstacle without losing base.

Add a dilemma round from mount. The top player may threaten armbar/triangle relationships or climb toward S-mount and armbar, but does not finish. The bottom player names which defensive priority is being stretched: neck protection, elbow connection, or angle. The round ends when one defensive response clearly exposes the other threat.

Add a height-read round. Before choosing an escape, the bottom player names the top player's height out loud: low mount over the hips, or high mount up the chest. From low mount they may attempt the bridge and roll or the elbow-knee escape. From high mount those are off the table, so they must turn to a side and go for a leg entanglement, a guard recovery, or the back door as the top player climbs. The bottom player can lose the escape and still do the drill correctly, as long as they stop running an escape that the height does not support.

## Back Control

Start from back control with seatbelt and hooks or body triangle, depending on the room's normal practice. For thirty seconds, the attacker may not choke. They may only separate a defensive hand, trap an arm, or maintain shoulder control. The defender may only protect the neck, recover inside hand position, and move the shoulders toward alignment.

Then allow normal finishing and escaping. The second round should feel different because both partners understand what had to happen first.

Add a control-choice round for the attacker. Name the objective before the legs move: follow, hold, or open and isolate. Follow with hooks, hold with the body triangle, and open with post control. Switch the lower body only when the objective changes. The habit being trained is to stop marrying one lower-body configuration and start matching it to the job.

Add a direction round for the defender. Name the two turns separately. The jaw turns toward the choking arm to protect the neck; the shoulders and hips turn toward the attacker's weakest alignment to leave the position. Then chase the single cue: replace their chest with the floor by getting a shoulder blade flat to the mat, and get the hip outside the knee line. The round ends when the defender can name which connection, upper or lower, they broke first.

Add a convert round. The attacker holds the back and calls the connection out loud: compressive when the defender is held between chest and legs with the seatbelt quiet, tensile when the seatbelt has become a strap the arms are hanging from. The instant it turns tensile, the attacker must convert instead of squeeze, following the escape to technical mount, mount, or turtle while the hips are still theirs. The defender wins by making the back go tensile. The attacker wins by converting before being towed into guard.

## Leg Entanglements

Start with a basic straight ankle lock or basic heel-hook control that both partners understand and that is appropriate for the room's rules. Before moving, each player names what is trapped: foot, knee line, hip, posture, base, or rotation.

The entangling player wins by off-balancing or improving control before attacking. The defender wins by freeing the correct line before turning, standing, or spinning. Keep the pace controlled. Leg entanglement drills should teach clarity, not surprise.

Add a heel-hook defense round, kept slow and with clear communication. The defender works the cascade in order: beat the foot first (move in and out, connect the feet or knees so the heel cannot be isolated, get a foot to the floor), then the knee (clear the knee line so the rotation has nothing to torque), then the grips (hand-fight the attacker's control and close the distance to the upper body). The attacker studies the other side of it: the moment the pin is lost, either reacquire it or read the loss as the signal to come up and pass, the held leg still a handle. Because the feedback arrives late, recognition ends the round, not pain, and the attacker owns the partner's safety.

## Takedowns

Start standing with grips. Before any attack, each player states the go/no-go read out loud: do I have dominant grips, and can I affect their posture more than they can affect mine? Only then choose to shoot, throw, pull guard, or keep hand fighting, and name why from the opponent's posture: head height, elbow and strike-zone coverage, arms, and square-versus-staggered stance. Keep it slow and controlled. The standing exchange is where people get hurt most, so the reset is the takedown secured without force, not the biggest throw.

## Turtle

Start with one player turtled and the other on top, grounded rather than circling. The bottom player protects two things only, the strike zone and the neck, elbows tight and hands high. The round resets on a leak: a flared elbow opens a hook and the back, a dropped and controlled head opens the front headlock. The top player names which direction the leak just handed them and reads it from the bottom player's posture. Elbows tight over the hook lanes free the collar and the back. High elbows defending the neck free the hook entry.

## Front Headlock

Start with one player in a front headlock, the other's head controlled and below the hips. First read the strangle. The attacker names the three variables before working: which part of the arm meets the carotid artery, the bicep or the wrist and forearm; where the grip is locked, outside the trapped arm or on the neck side; and what the body is doing, rolling, sitting, or standing. Remember both close one carotid artery directly and drive the deltoid into the other.

Then read the defense by arm position. With the arm out, the defender hand-fights the choke arm first, and reaching for a leg while the neck is trapped loses the round. With an arm-in guillotine, the defender jumps to the far side, over the top, so the body lands across the attacker's and the trapped shoulder wedges the neck free. Against the anaconda or D'Arce, taken from the top, there is no jump across; the defender wins earlier, by denying the arm-and-neck connection before it locks and keeping the head from being driven below the hips. In every version, name the destination out loud: get the head back over the hips.
