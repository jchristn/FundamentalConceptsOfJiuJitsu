# Rules and Safety Note

Jiu jitsu is practiced under many different rules. An academy round, an IBJJF gi match, an ADCC-style no-gi match, a submission-only event, an MMA context, and a self-defense class may all reward or forbid different choices. A technique that is legal for one adult division may be illegal for another rank, age group, uniform, or event.

Do not use this book as a rulebook. Use it as a conceptual guide. Before competing, check the current rules for the event, division, belt, age group, and gi or no-gi format. Before training higher-risk mechanics, follow the standards of your instructor and room.

Safety also cannot be reduced to legality. Some legal techniques can be applied recklessly. Some illegal competition techniques may still be studied safely in the right room under direct supervision. In training, the line depends more on whether both partners understand the danger, the pace, the escape options, and the tap than on whether something is "allowed" or "not allowed."

Submissions should be controlled enough that the defender receives information before injury. The attacker should know what structure is at risk and apply pressure with care. The defender should tap early enough that pride does not turn a lesson into time off the mat. Late-stage joint locks, rotational leg attacks, neck attacks, spinal pressure, jumping attacks, and high-amplitude takedowns all deserve extra attention because the cost of a mistake can be high.

We are all training partners. Everyone is here to get better, and the way we do that is by making each other better. We all have jobs, families, and lives outside these mats. No one wants to live that life injured. Respect the tap. Apply submissions slowly and methodically. When you are off the mats, do whatever you like. In here, respect your training partner. They are the whetstone that refines you.

No escalation. That is the room standard before any specific technique is discussed. A student who has lost a position should have enough humility to accept the lesson instead of turning the round into an injury contest. Hard training can still be playful, competitive, and uncomfortable. Malice does not belong in it.

In practice, no escalation means immediate de-escalation. When a round starts getting heated, stop, find new partners, and let heads cool down. It is on all of us to protect the training environment before pride turns into an injury.

If someone catches me in a legitimate submission, I tap and congratulate them. Afterward, if they ripped the submission or applied it too quickly, I will tell them plainly that they risked health and injury by doing it that way. Winning the exchange does not excuse losing control of the finish.

Wrist locks belong under the same standard. Go for them, but do not rip them. A wrist lock may appear quickly and feel almost funny in the room, but respect the training partner and give them the same care you would want from them.

Higher-risk submissions require even more discipline. I teach dangerous submissions, including attacks on joints that are less resilient, even to white belts, but control comes first. The student should understand how to control the position before they think about finishing. Once control is established, the mechanics of the submission can be shown clearly, and in the gym the finish should often be catch and release. The point is to understand the danger without needing to prove it on a teammate's knee, heel, neck, or spine.

This book's leg-lock discussion stays focused on the basic straight ankle lock and the basic heel hook. Other variations, including inside and outside control, backside 50/50, and related positions, come up only as context, but the safety standard remains the same: control first, mechanics second, no surprise finishes, and catch and release when the joint is less resilient or the feedback arrives late.

I do not practice one specific competition rule set as the law of the room, but the basic jiu jitsu point system is still worth understanding: two points for takedowns, sweeps, reversals, and knee on belly; three points for passing the guard; and four points for mount, back mount, or back control with hooks. Points are not the purpose of this book, but they reveal what the sport tends to reward: getting on top, passing the legs, controlling dominant positions, and making the opponent solve increasingly worse problems.

Some techniques are intentionally not part of normal instruction. We do not teach slams or jumping guard. We do not teach neck cranks as a goal. We teach students to apply proper chokes, while also making clear that a neck crank can be effective and is not what we want to do to training partners. We intentionally avoid spinal pressure techniques, though the can opener from guard can be taught as a concept with care. Standing work is common in drilling, flow rolling, and live rolling, but the goal is always to get the other person to the mat without injury.

Good mechanics and good safety are not opposites. Better isolation, cleaner leverage, and more precise alignment should make training calmer. A student who understands where the danger lives should need less force to demonstrate it.
