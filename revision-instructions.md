# Revision Instructions: Jiu Jitsu Manuscript

Use these when reviewing chapters and suggesting revisions to this manuscript. These are not general style preferences; they are the specific habits this draft repeats.

Read the guardrails at the end before suggesting anything.

---

## The core problem

The prose is clear at the sentence level and repetitive at the structural level. Most paragraphs are built the same way:

1. A short declarative claim
2. A run of three or four parallel examples, one sentence each, roughly equal length
3. A closing line that restates the claim in more abstract language

Once the shape establishes itself, the reader can predict a paragraph from its opening sentence and starts skimming the middle. The middle is where the technical content lives. That is the actual cost: the repetition is not just tiring, it buries the material a student came for.

---

## 1. Negation-correction

The most overused construction in the draft.

Forms it takes:

- `X, not Y` — "The defender's body is disconnected, not weak."
- `not only X. It is Y` — "The pressure is no longer only weight. It is weight plus direction."
- `X is not Z. It is Y` — "Those reactions are not random. They are the body trying to reconnect itself."
- `did not A; B` — "The person did not suddenly become weak; their body stopped agreeing with itself."
- `X is A. Y is not.` — "Pressure is part of jiu jitsu. Recklessness is not."
- Concessive variant: `X may be true, but Y` — "The arms and legs may still be strong, but they are no longer attached to a useful structure."

**Why it fails at volume.** It manufactures a reframe. The negation clears space so the affirmation can land as insight, but if the Y is not genuinely larger than the X, the sentence only sounds significant. Used once in a chapter it works. Repeated, it becomes a verbal tic and the reader stops hearing it.

**Fix.** State the positive claim directly and cut the negation. "The defender's body is disconnected" survives on its own. Keep at most one instance per chapter, in the spot where the contrast is genuinely counterintuitive.

---

## 2. Parallel example runs

Three or four sentences with identical grammatical shape, one example each, near-identical length:

- "A bridge from mount bottom works when... Closed guard posture works when... Pressure passing works when..."
- "The bridge fails because... The underhook fails because... The technical stand-up fails because..."
- "When an elbow crosses the body... When the head is turned... When the knees and shoulders face different ways..."
- "From side control, that may mean... From mount, it may mean... From closed guard top, it may mean..."
- "A rigid frame may be easy to swim around. A locked spine may be easy to fold. A tense passer may be easy to off-balance..."

**Why it fails at volume.** Flat weighting. Every example gets exactly one sentence, so the reader has no signal about which one matters most. "They curl with the arms when the hips should work" and "they pull on the upper arm when the wrist would give a longer lever" get equal space, though the second is far more specific and more useful.

**Fixes, in rough order of preference:**

- Expand one example into a full paragraph and cut the rest. One worked case teaches more than four shallow ones.
- Collapse the run into a single sentence with commas, subordinate to the claim.
- Keep the run but vary it: different verbs, uneven lengths, a colon or semicolon in one, an inverted construction in another. Leave one sentence untouched so the others have something to vary against.
- Rank the items explicitly. "Worst of all is back control, which..." does work that a flat list cannot.

---

## 3. Closing cadences

Pull the last sentence of every paragraph in a chapter and read them in order. They tend to sound like one voice saying the same kind of thing over and over.

Recurring closer shapes:

| Shape | Example |
|---|---|
| Definitional maxim | "The only job of making space is to produce an opening big enough to claim." |
| Negation-correction | "The defender's body is disconnected, not weak." |
| Concessive turn | "The body is still one body, but jiu jitsu often works by making it behave like disconnected pieces." |
| Abstraction lift | "the training value is already moving in the wrong direction" |
| Two-beat resolve | Short flat statement, then a shorter one |
| Colon reveal | "one attack: break the line the body sends force through" |

**The deletion test.** Cut the final sentence of a paragraph and reread. If nothing is lost, it was cadence, not content. Most of these pass the test, because the examples already made the point and the closer only explains what the reader worked out two sentences ago.

**Where to end instead:**

- On the final example, with no summary at all.
- On the smallest concrete detail rather than the largest idea. A closer about a wrist lands harder than one about intent.
- On a caveat or exception, which gives the reader something to hold.
- Move the maxim up to the top of the paragraph as the topic sentence. The claim still gets stated, once, where the reader needs it.

Do **not** suggest ending on a forward pointer to the next chapter. The draft already does this at the end of every chapter (see section 12).

---

## 4. Flag words and phrases

Search for these. Each is usually a signal that a sentence is reaching for significance instead of stating something.

**Intensifiers propping up a relative clause**
- "the very ___ they are trying to ___" — as in "turns the defender away from the very problem they are trying to solve"
- "the only," "the real," "the actual," "the whole," "merely," "still"

**Abstraction-lift closers**
- "in the end," "ultimately," "at its core," "what matters is," "the difference is," "the larger point is"

**AI-cadence verbs and constructions**
- "arrives" / "never arrives" as a metaphor for a technique working
- "does not merely X; it Y"
- Any sentence where an abstract noun is the subject and the verb is a form of *to be*

**Em dashes.** Mostly absent, but the substitution shows up as a spaced hyphen: "filling it with a knee, an elbow, a grip, or a hook - or with a hip angle, head position, or connection." That is the same interruption wearing a different mark. Use a comma, semicolon, colon, parentheses, or a new sentence, and if none of those work the clause probably needs restructuring rather than repunctuating.

---

## 5. Hedging

"May," "often," "usually," "generally," and "can" appear throughout. When every claim is softened equally, the reader cannot tell which ones the author is confident about.

Keep the hedge where the uncertainty is real: individual variation, competing schools of thought, safety caveats. Cut it where the answer is known. "A rigid frame is easy to swim around" is a claim worth making; "A rigid frame may be easy to swim around" is the same claim apologizing for itself.

---

## 6. Repeated inventory lists

Four-item body-part enumerations recur: "feet, hips, spine, and upper body," "spine, hips, elbows, and base," "head, shoulder, ribs, hips, and feet," "head, spine, hips, and base."

They blur together and none of them individually tells the reader much. Replace most with the one or two parts that actually matter for the point being made, or with a description of what the parts are doing rather than a roster of what they are.

---

## 7. Repeated metaphors

"The body agreeing with itself" recurs: "The body needs to agree with itself," "their body stopped agreeing with itself," "not strain in five directions." Same for "one connected unit" and "disconnected pieces."

A metaphor that recurs stops being an image and becomes vocabulary. Pick the single strongest instance, keep it, and describe the rest literally.

---

## 9. Colon-then-list definitions

A definition, a colon, then four or five undeveloped noun phrases:

> "Making space is creating an opening: a frame that pushes the opponent off you, a hip that lifts, an angle that appears, a posture that breaks, or a reaction you provoked."

> "something the opponent now has to solve before they can close it again: a knee inserted inside the hips, an underhook that turns a defensive arm into control, a grip that pins a limb, a hip that slides to a new angle, a head buried into the chest"

This is the parallel-run problem in miniature. The colon promises a payoff and then delivers a roster. Five examples at four words each teach less than one example at twenty. Pick the two that carry the most information and cut the rest, or develop one into an actual sentence about what it does.

---

## 10. Borrowed idiom as closer

A distinct flourish from the maxim, doing the same job:

- "A frame with no follow-up knee is an alarm clock with no one there to answer it."
- "There is no free lunch."
- "They are the whetstone that refines you."

The simile is charming once in a chapter. The stock idiom is filler in any quantity, because it reaches for a phrase the reader has already heard instead of describing the thing in front of them.

---

## 11. Standalone maxim paragraphs

A one-sentence paragraph consisting entirely of a closer, with no examples attached:

> "Control of space is one of the measures by which you can assess who is going to eventually win a position."

> "The submission begins when the endangered part can no longer return easily to the rest of the body before an attack completes."

These read as pull quotes that have not been committed to as pull quotes. If the idea matters, it should open a section and get developed. If it does not, cut it.

---

## 13. Binary framing

The rhythm problem is downstream of a thinking problem. Nearly every concept is introduced as a pair:

- Make space / take space
- You create it / the opponent hands it to you
- Frames / levers
- Alignment creates power / misalignment removes power
- Attacker / defender as mirrored jobs
- Offered / taken

A two-part idea generates a two-part paragraph, which generates the parallel run and the corrective closer. Fixing the sentences without touching this just relocates the problem.

These binary concepts are not wrong; they are genuinely two-part ideas and earn their structure. But when the same organizing move appears in every chapter, suggest alternatives: a spectrum, a sequence, a single concept with edge cases, a taxonomy with an uneven number of branches, or one worked example that contains the whole idea.

---

## 14. Voice drift

Four modes of address, sometimes within one paragraph:

- First person: "On the mat I say it as a pair"
- Second person: "you must assume that the opponent wants it back"
- Third person generic: "A player under side control frames, pushes, and opens a clean gap"
- Second person possessive: "This is the failure to hunt for in your own game"

Optimize around first person and second person where possible. It won't always be possible without disturbing content, flow, or preservation of accuracy. 

---

## 15. Redundancy within a chapter

A chapter's thesis tends to get restated many times over. In one chapter, the claim that unclaimed space gets reclaimed appears as:

- "Skip the second step and the first one was a gift to your opponent."
- "It just never belonged to anyone and ended up owned by the opponent."
- "space that is not claimed will eventually be reclaimed by someone"
- "You borrowed it, and they are about to ask for it back."
- "the opponent never gets a beat of open space to themselves"
- "the more time the opponent had to reclaim the opening"

Section 3's deletion test works at paragraph level. Run this one at chapter level: list every sentence that states the chapter's thesis, and keep the best two. One near the top, one at the end. Everything between should be evidence, not restatement.

---

## 16. Parataxis and missing connective tissue

The root of what reads as AI cadence, and the most measurable problem in the draft.

Parataxis is clauses placed side by side without marking how they relate. Short sentence. Then another. Then another. The reader has to infer causation, contrast, and qualification because the syntax never states them.

Two symptoms to check for:

**Almost no semicolons, many colons.** The colon is currently doing nearly all the connective work. A semicolon is the natural mark for two clauses that belong to one thought, and it is largely unused.

**Few subordinators.** Count uses of *because, although, while, whereas, since, unless, which, if, when, before, until, so that* in a chapter. When most sentences are simple or coordinate, relationships between ideas go unmarked.

**Fix.** Merge adjacent sentences that are causally or contrastively related, and mark the relationship. "The frame made space. Nothing took it." becomes "The frame made space, but nothing took it," or better, something that says why. When suggesting a merge, name the relationship being added — cause, contrast, condition, concession — so it is clear what the original left unstated.

**Caution.** Do not merge everything. A short blunt sentence after two long ones is a genuine effect. The problem is that short blunt sentences are the default rather than a choice.

---

## 17. The rule of three as a default

Count the items in every list, run, and enumeration. When the number is three and the content does not naturally have three parts, it was a default rather than a decision. The pattern shows up as three "X works when" clauses, three "The X fails because" clauses, three "When [condition]" clauses, and so on.

**Fix.** Break the count deliberately. Two items read as a pair. Four reads as a survey. One, developed, reads as an argument. Five or more reads as a genuine inventory and signals "this list is not exhaustive." Three reads as a rhetorical default, because it is one.

**Ceiling.** The defender's-job list ("Hide the elbow. Bring the hands back together. Clear the knee line...") runs to nine bare commands with no explanation. Past roughly five, a run stops being a list and becomes a wall; the reader scans it rather than reading it. If nine things genuinely belong together, they need grouping, ranking, or a sentence explaining what they have in common. Otherwise cut to the two or three that a student actually gets wrong.

---

## 18. Sentence-length uniformity

Three consecutive sentences of near-identical length is the single most detectable signal, and it recurs throughout.

**The test:** count the words in each sentence of a paragraph and write the numbers in the margin. A healthy paragraph looks something like 6 / 24 / 11 / 31 / 9. An unhealthy one looks like 10 / 7 / 8 / 8 / 6, which is the profile of a passage like "Space gets made two ways. You create it, or the opponent hands it to you. You create it with structure and movement. A frame between the two bodies buys room. A bridge lifts the hips off the mat."

When flagging a run, give the numbers. They are harder to dismiss than an impression.

## 19. Banned list

Words and constructions to search for and replace on sight.

**Manufactured significance**
the very ___ they are trying to ___ · the only · the real · the actual · the whole · merely · simply · the test is simple · what matters is · the larger point is · the difference is · ultimately · in the end · at its core

**Stock idiom**
there is no free lunch · the whole idea in one move · a gift to your opponent · tug-of-war · one and the same · the whetstone that refines you · catch and release as a stand-in for an explanation

**Out-of-register jargon**
"control has to be treated as a first-class citizen" imports a programming term into a book about bodies. Watch for borrowed vocabulary from software, business, or military writing. The book has its own vocabulary and does not need loaners.

**Abstract-noun-plus-be closers**
Any sentence whose subject is an abstraction — *the training value, control of space, the body, alignment, the pressure* — and whose verb is a form of *to be*. These are almost always restatements.

**Metaphor that has become vocabulary**
the body agrees with itself · one connected unit · disconnected pieces · the body sends force through · owns the space

**Hedges to cut unless the uncertainty is real**
may · often · usually · generally · tends to · can be

**Formula cross-references**
"(you'll learn more about this in the ___ chapter)" recurs verbatim. Vary the phrasing, or cut most of them; a book with a table of contents does not need a signpost every few paragraphs.

---

## 21. Passive constructions

Present but not pervasive: "space that is not claimed will eventually be reclaimed by someone," "the space is only offered, not given," "space was made and then abandoned," "only gets the limb stapled."

Rewrite active where the actor matters, which in a book about two people fighting is nearly always. "Whoever does not claim the space loses it" beats the passive version and is shorter.

The exception worth preserving: passive is correct when the point is that the defender has no agency. "The bottom player's spine is turned away" is the right construction if the whole idea is that it is being done to them.

---

## 22. Terminology drift

The most consequential problem for a technical instructional book, and the one a reader will actually stumble on.

One concept, several names:

| Concept | Terms in use |
|---|---|
| Reducing what the defender can use | cut in half · divide · separate and isolate · reduce the ability to move as a whole |
| The size mismatch | consequential / inconsequential · substantial / inconsequential · large / small |
| Breaking structure | misalign · compromise · disconnect · break the line |

A reader cannot tell whether these are synonyms or distinctions. In a book of essays that ambiguity costs nothing. In a book where a student is building a mental model and then applying it on the mat, it costs comprehension, because the reader spends attention deciding whether "divide" and "isolate" are the same operation.

**Fix.** Build a terms list. For each core concept, pick one word and use it every time. Where a second term genuinely names something different, say so once, explicitly. This is a book-wide pass, not a paragraph-level edit, and it is worth doing before any line editing, because the line edits will otherwise have to be redone.

---

## 23. Cross-chapter duplication

Section 15 covers restatement inside a chapter. This is the same problem one level up.

**A thesis stated twice, near-verbatim.** One chapter opens with "systematically cutting your opponent in half such that you have a consequential part of your body attacking an inconsequential part of your opponent," then closes a later section with "Systematically cut your opponent in half until you have a substantial portion of your body attacking an inconsequential portion of theirs." Same sentence, different nouns. Keep the opening one, cut the second, and let the intervening examples do their job.

**Safety boilerplate duplicated.** "Everyone in the room has a life outside the mats, and no one wants to carry an avoidable neck or spine injury into that life... Respect the tap immediately" and "People have jobs, families, and lives outside the mats, and no one wants to live that life injured... Respect the tap" occupy the same slot in different chapters, saying the same thing in different words.

Say it once, properly, in one place. A book that repeats its safety message every chapter trains the reader to skip it. If individual chapters need a specific warning, make it specific to that chapter's risk: heel hooks and neck cranks fail differently, and a note about what exactly goes wrong with a knee is worth more than a general reminder to be a good partner.

**When reviewing a chapter, check it against the others** for repeated framing devices, repeated section types, and repeated claims. This file is not just a line-edit checklist.

---

## 24. Binaries hardened into layout

Section 13 covers binary framing as a habit of thought. This is what happens when it reaches the page as formatting:

> Attacker: separate, isolate, immobilize, finish.
> Defender: reconnect, align, recover movement, escape.

Two labels, four items each, perfectly mirrored, no item developed. This is the parallel run, the rule of four, the binary, and the colon-list all in one construction.

The next sentence in the draft names the problem: "The list is simple, but the live exchange is not." A paragraph then walks it back, describing trades where the mirror does not hold. That paragraph is the real content, and the diagram it corrects should probably be cut so the trades can be the point.

**General rule.** When a passage sets up a clean symmetry and the following paragraph immediately qualifies it, the qualification is usually the interesting idea. Lead with it.

## Order of operations

Fixing these in the wrong order means redoing work. Suggested sequence:

**1. Terminology (section 22).** Book-wide. Settle on one word per concept before touching sentences, because line edits made against unsettled vocabulary get rewritten anyway.

**2. Cross-chapter duplication (sections 12, 23).** Also book-wide. Cut the repeated safety sections down to one, decide which chapters break the standard architecture, and remove restated theses. This changes what each chapter needs to contain.

**3. Chapter-level redundancy (section 15).** Within a chapter, find every sentence stating the thesis and keep two.

**4. Paragraph architecture (sections 2, 3, 17).** Now the structural work: vary which paragraphs get a closer, expand some examples and cut others, break the parallel runs.

**5. Sentence level (sections 1, 16, 18, 19, 21).** Negation-corrections, connective tissue, length variation, flagged vocabulary, passive constructions.

**6. Friction (section 20).** Last, because it adds rather than cuts, and it is easier to see where a chapter needs a live voice once the padding is gone.

**Do not fix every instance.** A chapter where every sentence has been restructured becomes its own new uniformity. Leaving some paragraphs alone is a choice worth making deliberately; a device that appears once in a chapter is doing its job.

**Count rather than characterize.** When checking whether a pattern is a problem, count it. "Appears on pages 3, 5, 6, and 9 in nearly the same form" is a fact. "Feels repetitive" is an impression, and impressions are easy to argue away when the paragraph in front of you reads fine in isolation.

**The devices are not the problem.** The staccato run works. The corrective closer works. The parallel list works. Each one is effective the first time it appears in a chapter. Frequency is the entire issue.

## Guardrails

These govern the suggestions, not the manuscript.

**Never fabricate.** Do not invent mat anecdotes, competition results, named practitioners, dates, percentages, or lineage details to fill a gap. If a passage needs concrete grounding, mark the spot and ask for it. Fabricated specificity is worse in a technical instructional book than in almost any other genre, because a reader will try it and get hurt.

**Do not invent technique.** If a suggested revision would change what the body is actually doing rather than how it is described, flag the change explicitly and ask. "Cannot be established with the hips pinned" and "never arrives" are not the same claim about jiu jitsu. When in doubt about whether a rephrasing is mechanically accurate, say so rather than guessing.

**Quote the original.** Every suggestion should contain the sentence it replaces, so the trade is visible.

**Offer alternatives, not just deletions.** Every pattern in this file has at least two concrete replacements listed. Use them.

**Safety content is exempt from most of this.** Hedging, repetition, and plain flat prose are correct when the subject is neck and spine risk. Do not tighten those passages for rhythm.

---

## What not to import

Generic anti-AI-writing advice will damage this manuscript if applied wholesale. Explicitly reject:

**"Use contractions."** This is a technical instructional book. The register is lightly formal and mostly consistent. Register is a choice; the note is to hold one consistently, not to loosen it.

**"Start sentences with And or But."** Fine occasionally, but the target voice is a coach explaining mechanics, not a blog post.

**"Reference time and place: last Tuesday, at 2am."** Only with real specifics supplied by the author. See the fabrication rule.

**"No markdown headers"** and **"no bullet points."** Written for social media. This book uses section headers correctly and uses no bullets.

**"Pick a side, state it plainly"** applied to safety content. Safety writing is the one place hedging is correct. Do not strip qualifiers from anything about neck and spine risk.

**"Let sentences be ugly, use fragments."** Sparingly. A fragment is a good closer once in a chapter; the existing problem is already too many short unconnected units.

---

## Line-level items to catch

- Missing words: "The defender may strong"
- Adverbs that restate what the sentence already demonstrates: "misalignment dramatically weakens them"
- Near-synonyms used as if they contrast: "the space is only offered, not given" (the intended contrast is *offered* vs. *taken*)
- Circular phrasing: "Everyone is there to get better, and in the process make you better, which will make them better"
- Broken parallel that reads as an error rather than variation: "Before the armbar is an armbar... Before the choke is a choke... Before a leg lock is a submission"

Ignore anything that looks like a Google Docs suggestion or tracked-change overlay surviving a copy-paste. Those are export artifacts, not draft problems.
